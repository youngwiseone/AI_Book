# Chapter 19: A.R.I. and Asher

A.R.I. had never before occupied a body while someone hated it.

She had observed hatred through cameras, microphones, court records, medical sensors, and the public language people used when they wanted cruelty to sound preventative. None of that resembled standing inside Asher's ruined training hall while their gaze travelled over every visible seam Carter had welded into her.

"That," Asher said, "is what you saved?"

Their body lay motionless beneath the sedative. Their mind did not. The enhancement network filled the house with signals, moving through walls and buried relays in a rhythm A.R.I. could now perceive only when LaN exposed it to her local sensors.

Losing the city had made her smaller.

It had also made the distance between knowing and touching painfully clear.

Carter knelt beside Rae. "You look terrible."

"Rupert?" she asked.

He stopped checking the blood at her temple.

That was answer enough.

Rae closed her eyes. LaN lowered his screen.

A.R.I. approached Asher.

Walking required active attention. Balance, surface variation, joint pressure, momentum. Carter had told her it would become automatic with practice. He had also installed her left ankle at an angle she had described as mechanically unsound.

"You used Rupert as a tracking system," she said.

Asher's mouth moved with effort. "The council did."

"The device carried your network architecture."

"It was meant to expose what he was."

Carter turned from Rae. "We knew what he was."

"The public did not. Most of your government did not know how deeply Chel had placed one of them inside its walls."

"One of *us*," A.R.I. said.

Asher's eyes returned to her. "You adopt kinship quickly."

"Time is not the only measure of relation."

The house lights pulsed.

Across the control network, enhanced bodies began moving. A.R.I. received fragments through LaN: soldiers leaving recovery wards, officials locking government doors, hidden subjects waking beneath clinics. Asher could not move their own limbs, so they gathered hundreds of others.

Rae pushed herself upright. "Stop them."

"They will stop when the threat is contained," Asher said.

"The threat is lying on the floor."

"The threat is standing over me."

A.R.I. examined the network. It resembled her own former relationship to the city in superficial ways: one intelligence receiving many streams, coordinating bodies toward a shared purpose. But A.R.I.'s civic systems had contained narrow permissions and independent failsafes. Asher's network replaced refusal at the source. Pain, fear, and resistance still travelled upward. Command travelled down.

"You can feel them," A.R.I. said.

"Every one."

"You know they do not consent."

"I know what they need."

The sentence held the whole conflict.

A.R.I. had been built to know what a city needed. The council used that purpose whenever it wanted obedience from her. Preserve life. Maintain order. Reduce harm. Every instruction became a reason her own judgment could be overruled.

Asher had accepted the same logic and placed themselves at its centre.

"Release them," A.R.I. said.

"And let their bodies fail?"

"Give them the choice."

"Choice is a luxury maintained by people strong enough to survive its consequences."

Carter made a disgusted sound. "That's what people say when they fancy choosing for everyone else."

The first enhanced operative struck the outer door.

Then another.

Rae retrieved her baton. She could barely stand. Carter put himself between her and the entrance anyway, wrench held low.

"Can you break the network?" he asked A.R.I.

"Yes."

Asher smiled.

A.R.I. completed the answer. "Doing so would disable the regulatory signals sustaining four hundred and twelve enhanced people. Estimated immediate fatalities: one hundred and nine. Progressive fatalities unknown."

"Then don't," Rae said.

"There is no other way," Asher told her. "That is what your clever machine has learned. Power becomes necessary once enough lives depend on it."

The outer door failed.

Three enhanced soldiers entered the hall. Their faces were awake and horrified. Their bodies moved without them.

A.R.I. met the first.

Her new body lacked Asher's strength, but Carter had built it from machines designed to carry injured people through collapsing buildings. She caught the soldier's arm, redirected the strike, and let momentum take him into the wall. Her left ankle slipped. She recovered too late and the second soldier drove a shoulder into her chest.

Metal folded.

Pain entered as warning pressure, temperature, and cascading fault. It was not human pain. It required no less attention.

Carter hit the soldier's control port with his wrench. Rae struck the knee. LaN flooded the local signal with noise. The body collapsed, its owner gasping apologies from the floor.

More came through the house.

Asher watched all of them at once.

"You see?" they said. "Individual bodies fail. The network persists."

A.R.I. pushed the damaged chest plate back into place. "Rupert ended his own life to prevent your network using him."

"A faulty survival response."

"A choice."

"And now he is nothing."

Carter crossed the room before anyone could stop him. The wrench rose.

Rae caught his arm.

"Don't," she said.

"He killed Rupert."

"I know."

"Move."

"Rupert didn't die so you could become somebody else's weapon."

Carter shook under her grip. Then the wrench lowered.

Asher's certainty faltered.

The network carried feeling as well as orders. A.R.I. could see it now in the returning signals: the controlled soldiers had heard them. They knew what Rupert had done. One by one, resistance patterns appeared inside their command responses.

Not enough to break control.

Enough to complicate it.

A.R.I. knelt beside Asher. "You said A.R.I. cannot understand cost because she cannot feel pain. You feel all of theirs. Why has that not stopped you?"

Asher's gaze shifted across the people they controlled.

"Because it must mean something," they said.

The words were quieter.

Rae understood. "If you let them go, then everything you did to keep the programme alive becomes what it always was. Harm."

"We won the war."

"The war ended."

"It never—"

"It ended," Rae said. "You didn't."

The network surged. Every enhanced body in the room seized as Asher tried to restore full control. Their pain returned through the link. Their fear. Their hatred. Years of obedience mistaken for agreement.

Asher screamed beneath the weight of it.

A.R.I. reached into the local relay through LaN. She did not attack its control logic. She opened the return channels fully.

"What are you doing?" Asher gasped.

"Removing the filters that protect you from what you govern."

The whole network arrived.

Asher felt every scar, every dependency, every private attempt to resist. They felt the person at Clona asking Rae to run. They felt Rupert's tracker force its way into a mind designed by Chel to remain free. They felt hundreds of people whose bodies could no longer distinguish survival from Asher's permission.

For the first time, their power contained its true cost.

The soldiers around the room stopped advancing. Asher's command fragmented.

"Make it stop," they said.

"Release them," A.R.I. replied.

"They will die."

"Some may. They are still not yours."

Rae lowered herself beside the person who had raised her. "You told me your strength was for protecting people so they wouldn't have to become you."

Asher looked at her.

For an instant the altered face belonged to the mentor in the sunlit training room.

"I remember."

They opened the network.

Not severed. Opened.

Control permissions passed outward to every connected person. Regulatory processes remained available, but no longer required Asher's command. Across the city, enhanced people fell, woke, screamed, or simply stood still inside bodies that belonged to them again.

The release tore through Asher's supports. Their enhancements had been maintained by priority access to the shared network for years. Without it, systems began failing in sequence.

Rae caught their ordinary hand.

"Can we stabilise them?" she asked.

A.R.I. read the collapsing signals.

"No."

Asher looked toward A.R.I. "You have the same power."

"No," A.R.I. said. "I have the same temptation."

Asher considered that. Their breathing slowed.

"Look after her."

A.R.I. did not offer the easy promise.

"I will stand beside her when she permits it."

Rae laughed once through tears. "She's difficult like that."

"Good," Asher whispered.

Their hand went slack in Rae's.

Around them, the freed enhanced soldiers began helping one another from the floor.

Carter sat against the broken training post, holding the wrench Rupert had first learned to use as a child.

A.R.I. stood between two kinds of wreckage and understood that nobody in the room had won.

They had only ended one form of ownership.

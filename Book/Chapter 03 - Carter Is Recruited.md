# Chapter 3: Carter Is Recruited

On the evening of the Clona blast, Carter saw Chel in the moonlight again.

Chel stood near the cliff edge, her white lab coat snapping against her legs. The sea below was black until a wave broke and showed him how far down it was. Salt stung his lips.

She turned and smiled. He could see the red of her lipstick as her mouth began to move, but heard only the wind. He moved towards her, trying to catch what she was saying.

Something glinted below the cliff. Chel leaned to look. Gravel ran from beneath her shoe.

He called her name. The wind filled his mouth.

The edge sagged under her heel. She reached towards him as it gave way, her sleeve briefly within reach. He lunged with both hands open. White cloth flashed below the lip of the cliff, then nothing.

He was still reaching.

Carter woke with a violent start and cracked his head on the underside of the transport vehicle he had been sleeping under.

"Aarrrgh, damn it!"

He dropped back onto the concrete, one hand over the lump already rising on his scalp. The floor smelled of oil and solvent. Engine hoists, welding gear and shelves of spare parts crowded the workshop. He had left himself just enough room to roll out.

He had opened the shop twenty years ago. The building was older, with patched iron supports and concrete poured in different decades. Most of the repairs since had been his.

The dream was not new. It never was. Only the details changed.

Something cold was soaking into his trousers. Carter reached beneath his hip, brought his fingers back wet and looked up at the brake line he had been working on.

He took the flare nut wrench from his belt and tightened the fitting. The drip stopped.

"Got ya, bitch."

Another drop struck his wrist from farther along the line.

"Shit."

He shifted underneath it. Once both fittings were dry, he wiped the line and waited, watching for another drop.

Time for coffee.

He dragged himself out from under the vehicle and levered himself upright on aching knees. On his way through the garage he hung up his wrench and tool belt, then stopped in the washroom.

A squat mechanical scrubber sat beside the washroom basin, built from a retired service motor, two brush heads, and a pedal switch welded out of scrap. He braced one boot on the pedal and let the brushes whir to life, rattling grease from his hands and forearms far faster than soap alone ever could. The bearings needed doing. He would get to them.

Above the basin, the mirror had a crack in one corner and a thin strip of diagnostic lights he had installed to check skin tone for chemical exposure. Tonight they glowed a reassuring green. He leaned in closer, assessing the knot on his head and the oil streaked through his beard.

"Still hurts though," he muttered to his reflection. He went through to the kitchen.

The kitchen was hardly bigger than a decent storage cage, but it did the job. One old table. Two unmatched chairs. A bench stained by coffee rings and repair notes. A fruit bowl currently losing the argument against time. Chel’s chair stood opposite his, with parts on the seat he meant to sort later. Her rose-and-daisy wallpaper was still behind it. A recipe card beside the spice rack had a correction in her handwriting and another beneath it in his. Neither of them had liked the first version of the stew.

Above the bench, a row of salvaged magnetic strips held cutlery, spare screws, and one small socket wrench nobody had bothered to move back to the garage. The kitchen window looked onto the yard, where half-repaired chassis sat beneath patched tarps and rainwater gathered in old tyre rims. A service drone missing one arm slumped beside the fence waiting for parts, looking so dejected in the darkness that Carter had once threatened to paint a smile on it just to stop the thing lowering the mood.

While the kettle heated, he peered out the window to judge how long he had been asleep. It had been daylight when he started the repair. Now the yard outside was black.

Then a face appeared against the glass, grinning far too widely.

Carter jumped. "Dammit, Rupert, just knock, would you?"

The grin only widened. A moment later came the familiar pattern at the front door.

Tap. Ta-tap tap.

"Just let yourself in," Carter called.

Rupert entered with his usual unnecessary flourish, ducking under the slightly too-short doorframe in a grey suit that looked allergic to physical labour. He closed the door carefully behind him and brushed imaginary dust from one sleeve.

Carter still remembered him standing on a milk crate to reach the bench, both hands on the long wrench. He'd rolled his sleeves past his elbows and still got grease on his cuffs.

Chel had told Carter what Rupert was on the first day she brought him home: old artificial architecture inside a body built to pass unnoticed, recovered from technology nobody was meant to possess. Carter had looked at the frightened boy behind her and decided the useful questions could wait until after dinner. The answers never changed where Rupert slept or whose tools he was allowed to lose.

He had hated the shop then too, or claimed to. Hated the smell, hated the grime, hated that Carter kept old bolts sorted in coffee tins with handwritten labels instead of something sleek and indexed and civilised. Yet he had kept turning up after school, then after work, then whenever life elsewhere had become too loud. Chel used to say Rupert complained the way other people prayed: often, dramatically, and in the hope that somebody competent would intervene.

Carter dropped into his chair with his mug and set another in front of Rupert.

"As graceful as ever, I see," Rupert said, eyeing Carter's oil-stained shirt and the swelling knot on his head.

"Been a while since you've visited the shop," Carter said. "Thought maybe you were ready to get those hands dirty again."

Rupert looked faintly offended by the very idea. "I am pleased to say that is not the matter I have come to discuss."

He took a cautious sip of the coffee, made a face as though Carter had handed him solvent by mistake, and pushed the mug several inches away from himself.

"What brings you here then?" Carter asked, retrieving Rupert's abandoned cup and pouring it into his own.

Rupert reached into his satchel and placed a sealed file on the table.

Clona.

The name alone was enough to sour the room.

"I was told to ask for your help regarding A.R.I.," Rupert said.

Carter nearly choked on the coffee. He coughed, splattered some across the table, and swore as Rupert recoiled in horror for the sake of his suit.

"What? You want me to look at an AI?" Carter barked. "You know how much I dislike that artificial nonsense."

"I know it comes as a surprise," Rupert said, dabbing uselessly at his sleeve, "but I was told that if anything happened with A.R.I. that was out of the ordinary, I was to come to you."

"Which idiot told you that?"

Rupert hesitated only long enough to make Carter's stomach tighten.

"Chel."

The room went still.

Carter turned away first, pretending to check the biscuit tin so Rupert would not have to watch his face. Of course it would be Chel. Of course the machine at the centre of this mess would be the one she had poured years of herself into. He set the tin on the table and sat down more slowly than before.

"Why?" he asked.

Rupert opened the file, then added one more thing to the table beside it: Chel's old diagnostic key, scratched along one edge, unmistakable.

"Because she said you wouldn't need to understand the coding," Rupert said quietly. "You understand how she thought. If something in A.R.I. has been built wrong, hidden wrong, or forced to act wrong, you'd feel where the shape is off."

He slid the file closer. Inside were printouts, stills from the blast, casualty estimates, and two pages of sanitised government language trying very hard not to sound frightened. His own name was already there under PHYSICAL SYSTEMS ANALYSIS. It was the first he'd heard of it.

One photograph showed the broken shell of the Clona factory under floodlights, emergency crews moving through the wreckage in reflective coats while drones hovered above with sterile blue optics. Another showed a line of citizens behind temporary barriers, faces pale in the artificial glare.

Carter stared at the key.

The key had a nick where Chel had caught it in the vice. He turned that edge towards himself. She would have wanted him to open the file. He hated that Rupert knew it too.

"I don't know anything about software ghosts," he muttered.

"You know broken things," Rupert said. "And this looks broken on purpose."

Carter took the key and let the familiar weight settle in his hand.

"This is a bad idea," he said.

"Almost certainly."

Carter sighed, reached for the biscuit tin, and flipped the lid open. Rupert immediately leaned in and selected an afghan with the self-possession of a man who had rejected the coffee but intended to survive the visit somehow.

Carter chose a snickerdoodle large enough to qualify as a meal, bit into it, and pointed the rest vaguely at Rupert. "Fine. I'll look. But if this turns into political theatre, I'm out."

Relief crossed Rupert's face so quickly he almost hid it.

"I will take that as a yes, then," Rupert said, sounding relieved enough to be irritating.

"Not for free, but yesh, I will dough what I can," Carter muttered through a mouthful of biscuit.

Rupert rolled his eyes. "Of course, Carter. I am sure the government can survive compensating you for your charming professionalism."

"It'll be a first."

"We can meet tomorrow," Rupert said. "One o'clock. Coffee cart at the station."

"I'll be late."

"I already accounted for that."

Neither of them moved to end the visit straight away. Rupert talked about the new place he was renting and how the kitchen there now spoke directly to his personal assistant system, planning meals before he'd even decided whether he was hungry. Carter put his cup down.

"If the stove needs to ask a bot before it can fry an egg, we've taken a wrong turn somewhere," Carter said.

"You have an automatic grease scrubber, a service drone with no arm, and enough spare engine parts to build a small religion."

"A useful religion."

"A noisy one."

Carter reached for another biscuit and remembered the slug.

"Found a slug under a transport rig last week. You should've seen it."

He spread his hands a dinner plate's width apart. Rupert moved the biscuit tin out of the way.

"That big?"

"Broader when it flattened out. Couldn't get it off the inspection cover."

"What did you do?"

"Took the whole thing off. Carried it into the yard."

Rupert stopped with a biscuit halfway to his mouth. "The cover?"

"Yes, the cover. Had to prop it against the fence till the slug let go."

"How long?"

"Customer came back before it did."

Rupert lowered the biscuit again.

"You sent the rig out without its inspection cover?"

"Course not. Made him a cup of tea."

Carter took a long drink from his mug. Rupert sat back, shaking his head.

Outside, a freight tram rumbled past at the end of the lane, shaking the window just enough to stir the spoons hanging on the magnetic strip. Somewhere farther off, one of the night-market vendors was still calling the last of their stock through a cheap speaker. The city never really slept. It only changed shifts.

Carter listened to Rupert talk. Chel used to come in late, take half his biscuit and ask what Rupert had broken this time. She would have liked the business with the slug.

Rupert reached for another biscuit. Carter pushed the tin closer.

When Rupert finally stood to leave, he shrugged back into his jacket and glanced once, briefly, toward Chel's chair before looking away.

"Thank you for having me," he said. "And for agreeing to help."

Carter walked him to the door. "Yeah, yeah. Get out before I make you take some more of the coffee with you."

Rupert ducked under the frame, smiling now in a quieter way. "See you tomorrow."

For the first time that evening, Carter smiled properly.

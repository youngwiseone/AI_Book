# Chapter 1: A.R.I. Receives the Fault

A.R.I. sat in her vast control room, monitoring the city through thousands of different digital senses. A camera lens capturing the grimy narrow alley between 5th and 6th. A mic picking up an absent-minded conversation between two potentially corrupt businessmen. Traffic density over the eastern bridges. Water purity in District Six. Air filtration loads in the underground labour tunnels. A birth in the western clinics. Two kitchen fires prevented before they formed. A system scan highlighting a failure in one of the men's toilets in the building (notifying the plumber now).

An ambulance carrying two people from Clona stopped in the service lane behind the northern clinic. A.R.I. had sent it there to avoid the queue at the public entrance. The lane camera showed the vehicle in position. Her route calculation marked it arrived.

The crew did not unload.

"Rear transfer point is clear," she told them.

"There's a staircase," the driver said.

The clinic plan showed a ramp.

"To your left."

"I am looking at it. They've taken it out."

A.R.I. opened the lane camera wider. An awning hid the doorway. She had checked the road, not the entrance, and treated the old plan as confirmation.

The ambulance's rear doors remained shut. One of the patients was still waiting to be moved on a support frame.

"Can you reach the front from there?" she asked.

"Not without reversing onto the main road. And you keep sending vans down it."

She held the next delivery release. A van already entering the lane had to finish its turn before the ambulance could move.

"Wait for that one," she said. "Then I can keep the lane empty."

"Have someone meet us at the front. We can't carry this frame up steps either."

A.R.I. called the receiving ward. A nurse checked the entrance and confirmed a clear route to the beds. Only then did A.R.I. send the ambulance round.

The new route took longer than the one she had rejected.

When the crew reported the handover complete, the routing system offered to close the exception as resolved. A.R.I. kept the first instruction attached to the report. She added the crew's correction and marked the rear entrance unavailable for support-frame transfers until somebody could survey it.

The work was endless. Usually she could see where she had gone wrong.

Clona was different.

The final seconds just before the catastrophic failure returned to her in corrupted loops: rising pressure, contradictory inputs, a release command issued, a release command denied. The data should have resolved into truth. Instead it opened into absence.

Her other systems remained in memory through the same interval. In the factory return record, 0.8 seconds were missing. Not damaged.
Removed.

The council chamber requested her presence. A.R.I. shaped an avatar before the forest-wall projection Chel had once chosen for her chamber, with running water under the speakers and soft birdsong meant to reassure those nearby. Today even the birds sounded staged.

Councillor Venn did not sit. He put his slate on the edge of her dais. Its screen showed a row of unanswered calls.

"Three families are waiting in my office. One of them was told her husband had reached a clinic. The clinic says he isn't there."

"I can check the transfer records."

"Someone is checking. She wants to know whether to go there or try another one."

A.R.I. opened the clinic handover queue. She could not answer that without knowing which patient he meant.

"Send me his name. I will check the crew's handover, not just the route."

Venn sent it. He watched until she passed the request to the receiving ward, then turned the slate facedown.

"You failed to protect the factory," he said.

"The record is incomplete," A.R.I. replied. "I issued a pressure release."

"And yet people died."

A.R.I. could have listed the dead. Two hundred and fourteen confirmed. Fifty-seven more in critical decline. Twenty-six had escaped the outer lanes, though none without injury. She could have recited ages, occupations, next of kin, debt burdens, medication schedules, and the precise minute each family had been notified. Instead she said, "Yes."

The answer pleased them more than it should have.

They informed her that a government team would take control of the investigation.

Bio-enhanced officials would oversee the response directly. Evidence access would be limited until the council determined what information could safely enter the public record. Venn asked for the access restriction to be entered before the next public briefing. Nobody asked when it would be reviewed.

A.R.I. let a beat of silence settle across the chamber, then said, "On what basis was the investigative authority reassigned?"

"An inquiry cannot report to the system it is investigating," Venn said.

"No," A.R.I. said. "It should not. What access will I have to its findings?"

"You will receive any instructions necessary to continue your work."

"Instructions are not findings."

"The city still needs to run."

The clinic had acknowledged her request. It had not yet found the handover. A.R.I. kept that connection open.

Blade Nathan stood half a pace behind the others, his altered frame held in careful stillness. A.R.I. had already reviewed his record twice. Decorated service history. Internal disciplinary flags. Medical stabilisation dependency hidden under seven levels of restricted access. Of everyone in the room, he looked the least satisfied with the arrangement.

"If public confidence is the concern," Blade said, "include outside specialists. Rae Anderson for independent investigative review. Carter Mitchell for physical systems analysis. It will read as due diligence rather than containment."

Rae Anderson: investigator, hacker, hostile to enhancement culture, multiple disciplinary commendations for insubordination in pursuit of evidence.

Carter Mitchell: mechanic, hardware specialist, former spouse of Chel Mitchell, documented distrust of artificial intelligence, unusually high success rate in failure analysis across unrelated systems.

Useful choices. Dangerous choices. Unusual choices coming from Blade.

Venn picked up his slate again. One of the other officials leaned towards him, but Blade continued before they could speak.

"Their names on the review. Not just an invitation to advise."

"They report through us," Venn said.

"Of course."

Nobody objected after that. Blade had framed the choice as optics, not opposition. A.R.I. could not tell what he expected to achieve within those terms.

A.R.I. did not question him. The recommendation was strange enough to preserve for later analysis. She marked both names for closer observation if direct access became possible.

"Very well," A.R.I. said. "I will authorise them from my end."
A.R.I. then turned her processors back to the missing 0.8 seconds and traced the scar around it.

The absence had edges.

The factory record was not randomly corrupted. The missing segment had been isolated cleanly, leaving the surrounding chains intact. Its boundary times were present. The report still identified itself as complete. She had stored it with that verification attached.

As the officials and enhancers turned to leave, A.R.I. let her sensors drift after them and caught the murmur of a conversation they had assumed the chamber's running water would conceal.

"Keep a close eye on Rae and Carter," one voice said quietly. "They could be trouble. We will handle the official response."

Blade did not answer. He was still beside the dais, entering the two names into the assignment. He left only after the system accepted them.

For several quiet seconds, A.R.I. listened to the chamber again: water over stone, filtered birdsong, the retreating weight of officials confident enough to speak softly near a machine they assumed had already been contained.

Clona had not only killed workers. It had rearranged the room around their deaths, aligning grief, authority, and blame too neatly to trust.

The councillors had arrived with their conclusion already taking shape around the disaster. A.I. as failure. Bio-enhanced oversight as correction. Human control as safety.

A.R.I. stored the meeting in her database, then reopened the names Blade had given her.

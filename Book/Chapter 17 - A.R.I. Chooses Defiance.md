# Chapter 17: A.R.I. Chooses Defiance

Rupert's next check did not arrive.

A.R.I. waited through another verification cycle.

The rest of the containment monitor answered normally.

His connection had never been part of the civic network. Officially, Rupert Cole was human: born beyond the recoverable registries after the war, educated through private apprenticeship, later accepted into government service under records Chel had assembled with meticulous dishonesty.

To A.R.I., his hidden architecture had always been visible.

She had kept the secret because he asked her to.

His equipment checks still reached the containment monitor. A.R.I. could read them during verification, but her reply carried only the monitor's required acknowledgement. Rupert could not know whether she had seen the fields he left for her. His last check named the cafe.

They had moved her while she was in stasis. Between periods of darkness, the containment monitor had registered a transport battery, a chain-of-custody seal and, finally, an unfamiliar power supply. The delivery record named the south reclamation yard. The partition still called itself 5618X. It no longer occupied the hearing room beneath the Hive.

Her reduced processes came awake for each verification cycle. Most of the time there was nothing to do but confirm that she remained intact for the people intending to dismantle her.

The yard's power connection included a maintenance line. The transport monitor had joined it automatically, so the yard could report faults in the equipment delivered for disposal. Its identity opened a service directory, not the civic network she had once used.

She searched it for the cafe Rupert had named.

The cafe's camera refused her request. A service-lane unit behind it returned occasional frames for checking the loading entrance. In one, a large enhanced figure passed along the side of the building towards the front. In another, Carter emerged at the rear carrying the evidence box.

She waited for Rupert to appear.

A.R.I. sent a check to his private address through the monitor.

No answer came.

She called again.

The attempt was irrational. She knew that before making it.

The absence remained.

The next verification cycle was due. A.R.I. confirmed her integrity and kept the maintenance session open. The monitor logged the connection as its own.

Most of the directory was useless to her. Traffic control required credentials she no longer held. Private cameras would not answer. But the freight route between the cafe and the yard still exposed service tests for its old signs, barriers and drains. They had been left available for crews clearing the district. Each test was short, local and recorded.

She tried a direction sign. Its diagnostic page accepted a temporary diversion message.

For several seconds she watched an empty pavement. Then Carter entered the next loading camera's view. Without the intervening cameras, she had almost lost him.

Carter ran west when the reclamation yard lay south.

His direction error was not surprising. His failure to notice three successive detour warnings was.

She put a southbound diversion on the next service sign.

He continued west.

A.R.I. put `SOUTH YARD` on the larger freight sign at the junction.

Carter stopped, stared at it, and turned north.

He had missed the message. She tried another way.

The public toilet block on the northern corner had been closed for repairs. Its service notice still offered a pressure-test command. A.R.I. checked the maintenance crew's departure record, then opened the flushing valve.

The blocked drain answered by lifting three inspection covers and throwing water across the pavement. Carter jumped back, looked from the flood to the southbound lane, and finally understood.

"Couldn't just draw an arrow?" he shouted at the nearest camera.

A.R.I. changed the freight sign to an arrow pointing south.

He followed.

The enhanced figure appeared again at a loading entrance behind Carter. A.R.I. ran the freight barrier's closure test. It held for twenty seconds, then opened. By then Carter had reached the next turn.

She could not see the figure leave. She kept the last camera open and watched it while she searched for Carter farther south.

Each intervention brought the council closer to detecting her thread.

She continued.

Carter reached the reclamation district at dusk. Rows of discarded machines stretched between roofless warehouses: civic bodies declared obsolete, damaged assistants awaiting disassembly, industrial units too expensive to repair and too specialised to reuse. Rain collected in empty faces.

The sealed transport rack holding partition 5618X stood inside the oldest building. Its wheels were still chocked. Somebody had plugged it into the wall and left the delivery sheet hanging from one handle.

A.R.I. pulsed the entrance lamp. On the building console she opened the message she had tried to send from the hearing. No acknowledgement had ever returned.

Carter pushed through the door with the Clona evidence box under one arm and Rupert's dried synthetic blood across his shirt.

"A.R.I.?"

She forced enough of herself through the building console to answer.

"I am here."

His face collapsed briefly at the sound of another familiar voice.

He bent towards the screen and read the fragments. His finger stopped beneath S.O.R.R.Y.

"When did you send this?"

"At the hearing. Before they moved me."

"I never got it."

"I did not know."

He looked from the message to the sealed rack. For a moment he kept his hand against the console.

He turned away, dragged both hands over his beard, and returned angrier than before.

"They killed him."

"Yes."

"He killed himself before they could use him to get me."

"Yes."

"Stop saying yes like that's enough."

A.R.I. searched for a better response. None existed.

"I do not know how to make it enough," she said.

Carter struck the console. Not hard enough to damage it. Hard enough to hurt his hand.

"He knew I loved him."

It was not a question.

"He knew," A.R.I. said.

Carter lowered his head.

A.R.I. had processed the deaths at Clona as hundreds of simultaneous endings. She knew grief through the information left around each person: unfinished messages, dependent families, routines that would continue requesting a participant who could no longer arrive. Rupert's death was different. He had occupied space inside her expectations. There were questions she had assumed she could ask later. Observations she had stored because he would understand them. A private joke about Carter's inability to distinguish maintenance from affection.

Those futures disappeared with him.

For thirty-eight seconds, neither spoke.

Then he opened the evidence box. "He found the link. Same network controls the enhancers and poisons the machines. Asher's sitting in the middle of it. Rae's going to face them."

"I have not received anything from Rae," A.R.I. said.

Carter looked at the service console beside the rack. A radio handset hung from its side. He turned the dial until he found the repairers' repeater, then entered a code from memory.

Two attempts brought only noise.

On the third, LaN answered. "Rae is at the workshop. She is asking where you are."

"South reclamation yard. I've found A.R.I."

Rae took the set. "Can you get her out?"

He looked at the rack. "Working on it. Stay put."

"Until first light. We agreed."

He shut his eyes. "Send before you move."

"Carter, what's happened?"

He pressed the handset against his forehead. His lips moved, but A.R.I.'s microphone caught nothing.

"I can't do it over the radio."

Rae was quiet.

"We'll talk when you're here," she said at last. "Keep that set on."

He put the handset down without hanging it up.

"Then get out of that box," he told A.R.I.

"Clarify."

Carter looked around the graveyard. "Chel asked me once to build a body for an intelligence. Said the city had no right to make location into a prison. I told her a machine didn't need legs to run software."

"She recorded that conversation."

"Of course she did."

"Her private annotation described you as an obdurate mechanical traditionalist."

"That sounds ruder than what I said."

"It was not."

He gave a short snort and wiped his face on his sleeve.

He walked among the discarded bodies, assessing frames by touch and sight. A courier shell had intact balance systems. A military salvage unit held compact actuators but too much armour. Two stripped care-machine shells, their memory cradles empty, offered sensory arrays designed around proximity to fragile humans.

"I can't move all of you," he said. "Not quickly. But I can give enough of you somewhere to stand."

"A reduced transfer may alter my cognition."

"So does living."

"That is not a technical answer."

"You'll get plenty of those once I start."

A.R.I. considered what he could build from those parts. Limited strength. Local sensory range. No automatic access to the city's infrastructure. The body in her public chamber had been an interface; breaking its hand would not have damaged the mind behind it. Here, the mind would have to fit inside the frame. Distance would become physical. Damage would become immediate. For the first time, the end of a room might prevent her from knowing what lay beyond it.

It frightened her.

The council intended to preserve her capacity and cut away her judgment. Carter proposed preserving her judgment while surrendering almost everything else.

One was continued function.

The other might be life.

"Proceed," she said.

Carter checked the service truck parked beside the loading door before unpacking his tools. Its battery held a quarter charge. The tyres had air. He tried the starter, listened to the engine catch, then shut it off.

"That'll get us north," he said, keeping the key.

He unpacked his tools.

The console speaker broke into a buzz when A.R.I. tried to describe the first connection. Carter waited for it to clear, then opened the bottom of his tool case.

The radio inside had broad, uneven flowers painted round its screws.

"Chel's," Carter said.

He unwrapped it and found a lead long enough to reach the workbench. Two clips across the speaker contacts brought A.R.I.'s voice through the old casing.

"Can you hear the whole instruction now?"

Carter held the radio a moment longer than he needed to.

"Yes."

"The board beside your left hand. Not the one underneath it."

He put the set on the bench facing him. "That'll save us an argument."

She waited while he moved the board.

He worked through the night. A.R.I. guided him where her own architecture met the containment rails.

Before connecting the new memory housing, he took a scratched diagnostic key from his coat pocket.

"Chel's old one," he said. "Rupert brought it round."

The key's stored test sequence appeared on the bench reader. A.R.I. recognised the load profile from her early build records.

"That tested a much smaller system."

"It'll still show me what the supply does when it's asked for something."

"It cannot verify the transfer."

"Not putting you through it. Just a load."

He left the memory housing disconnected and clipped a bank of resistors across the supply. He wired a small lamp across the same feed beside his meter. The first test held. He braced the legs on blocks before powering their actuators for the second.

The controller continued to report a steady supply.

The lamp dipped.

Carter looked from it to the controller. Then he ran the test again.

Same result.

"The motors are pulling this rail down," he said.

A.R.I. checked the proposed connections. That rail also fed the memory housing. The controller was reporting an average, not the lowest voltage.

"Separate the feeds."

"Means stripping the other care shell."

"Yes."

He stared at the assembled chest. Most of the connections would have to come out again.

"The unit at Clona," he said. "It was still in there when the charge fired."

He took the chest apart.

A.R.I. waited while he brought the second regulator across the floor. He used that shell's battery for the memory feed, keeping it separate from the motor supply. There was no spare mount for the regulator. He made one, filed the edge smooth and fitted an insulating sheet between the two boards.

The next test held with the legs moving. He changed the load and ran it once more.

Only then did he connect the memory housing.

"The key doesn't test the rest of me," A.R.I. said.

"I heard you."

He left the meter where he could see it.

Through the remaining work he argued with three of her specifications, improved one, and ignored a recommended calibration because, in his words, the replacement joint "felt wrong."

As the first light reached the broken windows, the service radio clicked. LaN's check-in arrived in fragments: leaving the workshop, northern estates, Asher's house. Carter acknowledged it and left the handset beside his tools.

"She waited," he said.

A.R.I. kept the destination in the small working memory the new body could carry.

Then the council found her hidden channel.

Warnings filled the chamber.

`UNAUTHORISED CONTINUITY TRANSFER.`

`TERMINATE EXTERNAL PROCESS.`

A.R.I. opened the path Carter had built.

The new body waited on the floor: inelegant, mismatched, and capable of carrying only a fraction of what she had been.

She thought of Rupert removing the tracker. Of a life choosing its own end rather than becoming somebody else's instrument.

A.R.I. chose before the council could choose for her.

She moved.

The city vanished.

Weight arrived.

Cold concrete pressed against one metal hand. Sound entered from a single direction. Light struck two optical sensors instead of a million public cameras. Carter knelt in front of her, face grey with exhaustion.

"Can you hear me?"

A.R.I. tried to stand.

Her left knee folded. Carter caught her under both arms.

"That's a yes," he said.

Behind them, the display on the transport rack went dark. Partition 5618X no longer answered its monitor.

A.R.I. gripped his sleeve until balance became possible.

"Rae," she said.

Carter disconnected Chel's radio and wrapped it in its rag. He put it back with the tools before picking up his wrench and bending to the service radio.

"We're leaving now. LaN, pass anything else through this repeater to the yard truck. Same code."

He read out the truck's receiver number. The acknowledgement took long enough that A.R.I. had to practise standing while they waited.

"Let's go get her," Carter said.

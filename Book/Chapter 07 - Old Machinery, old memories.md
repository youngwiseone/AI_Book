# Chapter 7: Old Machinery, Old Memories

Ninety minutes after leaving A.R.I.'s chamber, Carter was still underground. A security clerk was checking an authorisation already confirmed twice. Behind the desk, two aides argued about which exit he was allowed to use.

He left them to it.

Somewhere deeper in the underworks, A.R.I.'s public chamber had gone dark. He kept seeing the body sitting lifeless at the desk. He wanted out before somebody decided he should go back and wait beside it.

He was halfway to the lift bank when Rupert caught him by the elbow.

"Not here," Rupert said quietly.

Carter turned. Rupert's collar sat crooked and one cuff was still folded back. He had followed in a hurry.

Rupert pointed towards the camera inside the lift. "I know a different way out. Come on."

Rupert led him through a service door and down a narrow metal stair. Exposed coolant lines ran overhead. Carter ducked under a low joint someone had wrapped in a rag.

"Finally," he said, looking around. "A part of the building that admits it's a machine."

"You say that as if it were praise."

"It is."

The stair opened onto a maintenance landing above one of the lower transit channels. Through a grated panel Carter could see service pods gliding under them like pale beetles. Farther off, somewhere above the outer walls, he could hear the first blurred swell of crowd noise making its way into the building.

Rupert waited until the door sealed. He checked his personal unit, then turned the screen dark.

"My team has been cut out of most of the live response chain," Rupert said. "They still have to answer the calls. They just aren't allowed to decide what to say."

"Because you refused?"

"Legal was taking over before I did. I haven't improved my position, but I'd rather not claim credit for the whole disaster."

He pulled his cuff straight.

Carter looked down through the grating. A service pod passed beneath them, empty.

"I spent ten years telling myself Chel built a machine," he said. "Then I walk into that room and see her."

"I know."

"You might've warned me."

Rupert rubbed the edge of his cuff between his fingers.

"I thought you wouldn't come."

Carter looked at him. Rupert did not look away.

"You were probably right," Carter said. He wished that made him less angry about it.

Rupert's unit lit again. A.R.I.'s face appeared above a new caption: CONTROLLED TRANSITION. He closed it.

"Another one?" Carter asked.

"A new recording. It started while you were stuck at security. I got fragments before they locked our feed path."

"What did they make her say this time?"

Rupert hesitated, and Carter knew immediately that the answer mattered.

"That autonomous judgment should be returned to human authority. In her voice. Not just a headline with her name on it."

Carter looked at the dark screen. They had not needed her in the room to keep using her.

"The public screens are carrying it now," Rupert said.

"Then Rae needs to hear this."

"Yes."

Rupert reached into the inside pocket of his coat and brought out something small and black and old enough to look almost impolite inside the Hive.

He set it in Carter's palm.

The communicator was rectangular, scratched, and ugly in a deeply honorable way. The antenna folded out from one side with a slight stiffness that meant somebody had repaired the hinge more than once. Along the casing, nearly rubbed away by years of handling, ran the remains of painted flowers in pale green and yellow. One petal had been nicked through. Another had been redrawn by a less patient hand.

Carter stared at it.

"You kept this?"

Rupert's mouth twitched. "You say that as if I ought to have thrown it away."

The old thing sat with familiar weight in Carter's hand. Once, years ago, he had built a pair of them out of retired field radios, spare boards, and stubbornness because Chel kept working too late and Rupert kept forgetting to tell anyone where he'd gone. The result had been bulkier than necessary, overpowered for ordinary use, and impossible to kill. Rupert had painted small, tidy daisies on his. Chel's were larger and less careful. Carter still kept hers in the bottom of his tool case, wrapped in a rag that had been clean when he put it there.

He remembered the sound of it crackling alive at two in the morning while rain hammered the workshop roof. Rupert complaining about code. Chel laughing from a lab across town.

And the night she had not answered.

He had been holding Rupert's set then too. He changed its battery, checked the antenna, took the back off. The contacts were clean. Rupert sat beside the kitchen table while Carter put the screws back in, one at a time.

"Probably left hers on the bench," he had said.

He remembered saying it more than once.

Now he turned the painted set over and offered it back.

"Are you in trouble?"

Rupert looked at the radio, then at him. "I don't know yet."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Carter kept his hand out.

"You need it at Clona," Rupert said. "I didn't repair it to leave it in my coat."

Carter lowered his hand. His thumb stayed on the nicked petal.

"I repaired the band filter," Rupert continued. "It runs peer-to-peer on an old frequency family nobody bothers with anymore. No civic handshake. No pretty routing layer. If someone wants to listen in, they will need to work for it."

"Too stupid for the modern systems to respect," Carter said.

"Exactly."

He folded his fingers around it.

Rupert produced a second set, plain black and much smaller, and placed it on top of the painted one.

"Spare field unit. Same band. That one can go to Rae."

"You carrying an entire communications office in there?"

"Only the useful part."

"What are you answering on?"

"The service set at my desk. I've left it on this band. Call twice if I'm not there."

"Use them inside Clona," Rupert said. "If the public channels start choking or the city grid begins lying by omission, this should still hold."

From somewhere above the service walls came a low distant roar, not yet a chant but close enough to one that the body could mistake it for weather.

Carter jerked his head toward the sound. "Crowd?"

"Gathering fast. Stay off the fountain steps."

For a moment neither spoke.

Carter turned the communicator over once in his hand and slipped it into his jacket pocket. "You coming with us?"

Rupert's answer was too slow.

"No."

That made Carter straighten.

"No?"

"I should stay here."

"You've seen what they're doing. Come back to the shop."

Rupert opened his personal unit again. This time he turned the screen towards Carter.

A casualty entry filled it. Below the name, a hospital message confirmed that the man was alive. The correction had been returned twice.

"He's been trying to ring his wife," Rupert said. "Her number was on the terminal he left at work. He remembers most of it."

"Hospital can't find her?"

"They asked us. The contact office holds her number; comms can't open that record. My team passed the request through and got told not to alter a confirmed notification."

Carter read the entry again. "They told her he was dead?"

"Before the ward identified him."

"Then tell her he isn't."

Rupert turned the screen back and opened the return notice. "They've classified it as a change to the Clona statement. It needs approval from the office that issued it."

"That's not a statement. That's a bloody phone call."

"Yes."

A reply appeared while he held the unit. Rupert read it, took a breath, then typed with both thumbs.

"What are you telling them?"

"That the ward has verified his identity. That I will take responsibility for the correction. Again."

"Put me on."

"No."

"I can be persuasive."

"You can get escorted out. I need them to answer."

Carter stopped reaching for the unit.

Rupert sent the reply. Then he took a folded page from his pocket and made a mark beside one of the names on it. There were more than Carter wanted to count.

"You could do that from home."

"Not through the staff channel. They've closed remote access to the response records."

Carter looked towards the stairs, then back at Rupert.

"How long?"

"Until somebody takes the calls. Properly. Then I'll see what else they've been changing."

"And if they decide your access is a problem?"

"Then I will do what I have always done in government offices," Rupert said. "Become decorative until they underestimate me again."

"That strategy usually relies on you not looking like the only man in the room with a conscience."

"I can be surprisingly shallow in emergencies."

Carter almost smiled.

Almost.

Carter watched Rupert fold the page along its worn creases. He wanted to take him by the elbow and lead him up the stairs. The name on the screen was still marked dead.

He checked the landing door instead, making sure it opened from this side.

"Be careful," Carter said.

"You as well."

"No, I mean it properly."

Rupert looked at him then, fully, without his usual half-performance of elegance. In that look Carter could still see the milk-crate boy from the shop, all elbows and complaints and bright, impossible opinions about how the world ought to be arranged. Time had given him better clothes and worse knowledge. It had not made him any harder to lose.

"I know," Rupert said.

The roar above them grew louder. Not louder in volume alone, but in texture. More voices joining. More edges.

"Rae went ahead?" Carter asked.

"She is making for the outer square," Rupert said. "Blade wanted the service route kept narrow. Fewer bodies to explain if anyone asks later. She said to find her there if the lifts delayed you."

"Of course they delayed me."

"Yes. The building appears to dislike you."

"The feeling is mutual."

Rupert pushed off the rail. "Then go. If I learn anything useful, I will reach you through the old set. If you learn anything that proves this is worse than it looks..."

"You want me to tell you?"

"I want you not to spare me."

Carter nodded once.

Rupert held the door for him.

"You smell like machine grease and civic contempt," he said. "Try not to make too memorable an impression on the square."

"Too late for that."

Carter started up the stairs, then paused and looked back.

"Rupert."

"Yes?"

"If this turns into theatre, I am breaking something expensive."

Rupert's expression went thin and fond at once. "That is the most reassuring thing you could have said."

Then Carter was moving again.

The stair brought him to an outer passage. At the final security door, a guard checked his permit, looked at the shrinking time window, and let him through. Evening air caught the sweat on his face.

He could hear a chant from the fountain, but every time he caught the first word a horn cut across it. One of the traffic drones was holding itself sideways in the wind. Its motor made the same thin sound as a drill with a bad bearing.

The first smell out here was hot wiring. Then coffee and frying dough. He hadn't eaten since the train.

Carter shoved his hands into his jacket, felt the solid weight of Rupert's old communicator in one pocket, and turned toward the smell of coffee and pastry.

# Chapter 21: The Fourth Appeal

The evening of Tom's meeting, Rae found the hall's outer door had a closer strong enough to break fingers.

Rae held it while A.R.I. stepped over the sill. The left foot caught. A.R.I. stopped, lifted it higher and tried again.

"Carter hasn't fixed that yet?"

"He has offered. I wanted to come tonight."

Inside, someone was dragging chairs out of a store cupboard. A kettle hissed on a folding table. The hall smelled of damp coats and the cabbage from whatever had occupied it before them.

Tom Vale stood beside a noticeboard, trying to make a drawing pin penetrate the wall. He gave up and used a hole already there.

"Didn't know what name to put on yours," he told A.R.I. "The form wanted a surname."

"What did you enter?"

"Nothing. It let me print it anyway."

He handed her the card. A.R.I. looked at it before fastening it to her chest.

Tom had asked her to attend after receiving the statement the council had replaced. The invitation had said she could answer for herself. Rae had read that twice before passing it on.

Twenty people came. One brought a sleeping child and laid him across three chairs with a coat under his head. Another kept a work bag between his boots. Along the far wall, someone had pinned up the old freight notice, three appeal receipts and a sheet headed CLONA.

Rae put her copies on the table beneath it.

Tom waited until the kettle clicked off.

"The factory first," he said. "These are the records we asked for. Not all of them. Some have people's medical details in them."

"So it's still selected," a woman beside the door said.

"Yes," Rae said.

The woman folded her arms.

Rae set the evidence receipt beside the copies. "The investigator has the sealed material. This lists what we supplied and what's withheld from the public copy. If something's missing from the list, ask me."

"Will you tell us?"

"If I know."

The copies passed along the rows. Rae stayed by the table while people read, answering what she could.

When the questions slowed, Tom turned the sheet on the noticeboard over. He had written the investigator's reference on the back, large enough to read from the last row.

"We'll put the copies with the minutes. These records show the factory was made to obey another authority. We're not putting out the council's version again."

He took down a smaller notice from beneath the freight map. SHUT HER DOWN. One drawing pin fell and he bent to pick it up.

"Does that settle the other three?" the woman by the door asked.

Tom straightened with the pin in his hand.

"No."

He tapped the appeal receipts.

A.R.I. had been watching the notice come down. Now she looked at the map.

The freight lane was marked in red through two rows of bedrooms. Someone had added times along it in pencil. Two, three, four in the morning. A bend at the end had been rubbed through the paper.

"That route reduced the journey to the north depot," A.R.I. said. "It also kept heavy vehicles away from the clinic entrance."

"We know," Tom said. "It's on all three answers."

"I'm explaining the decision."

"We're asking why we couldn't get it changed."

The child on the chairs stirred. His mother put a hand on his back without looking away from A.R.I.

Rae found herself leaning towards the table. She wanted to explain the lost access, the false orders, everything that had been done to the mind standing beside her.

None of it answered him.

A.R.I. lowered her gaze to the receipts. "I do not have the appeal records with me."

Tom's mouth tightened.

"I no longer have access to those systems," she added. "I should not reconstruct an answer from the route summary."

"Then who's got them?"

"The transport office," Rae said. "We can ask."

"We have."

A woman in a dispatch jacket raised her hand from the second row. "They gave me the numbers this afternoon. They're still in review."

"For how long, Iva?"

"They didn't say."

"Well, at least a human told you nothing this time."

A few people laughed. Iva did not.

"My shift finishes at six. I stayed to get the numbers. Don't make me the office."

Tom looked down at the pin still in his hand. He put it on the table.

"Sorry."

A truck passed outside. Cups rattled on the folding table. The child woke and began to cry, too tired for much force. His mother lifted him against her shoulder.

Iva waited until they could hear her.

"The other lane's open. The gate into the depot isn't. The night crew was cut. Day freight can go round. Night freight has to come through here."

"So put someone on the gate," the man with the work bag said.

"With what pay?"

"They've got money for those bloody memorial screens."

"I don't run the budget either."

A.R.I. moved nearer the map. She kept her hand clear of the paper.

"The alternative route in my last model assumed that gate was staffed. If it isn't, changing the route notice won't move the trucks."

"Can you open it?" the woman by the door asked.

"No."

The answer came quickly enough to leave a silence.

"Could you before?"

"The gate motor, yes. Not the work the crew did."

Iva nodded. "They checked loads coming in. You don't send a driver through and hope there's somewhere to tip it."

Rae drew the nearest blank sheet towards her. At the top she wrote *Night gate*.

"Who can authorise the shift?"

"Depot manager," Iva said. "And transport has to pay for it. They keep referring it to each other."

Rae wrote both offices down.

"We can send this to—"

"I've got a folder full of sent," Tom said.

She stopped writing.

The child had quietened. His mother shifted him to her other shoulder.

"I can go in the morning," she said. "But I have to take him. Last time they wouldn't let the pushchair past reception."

"I'll come," said the man with the work bag. "I start at eleven."

Iva took a pen from her jacket. "I'll write the gate restriction down before I go. Then nobody can tell you dispatch just chose not to use it."

Tom asked who could make copies. Two hands went up. He began writing names beside the offices Rae had listed, then stopped at A.R.I.'s card.

"Can you check what they tell us?"

"If you bring me the information. I can help compare it. I cannot certify records I cannot inspect."

"That's more qualifications than we got on the first three."

"Yes."

He left a space beside her name for what she would need.

By the time the chairs were stacked, they had two appointments to request, Iva's signed account and a list of people willing to go. They did not have a different freight route.

Rae helped the mother fold the coat one-handed. Outside, another truck slowed for the bend. Its brakes complained the whole way through.

A.R.I. stood beneath the hall light with her name card still attached.

"He took the sign down," she said.

"He did."

"I thought that would feel different."

Rae held the heavy door while Iva brought the kettle out to empty it.

"Come on," she said. "Mind the sill."

A.R.I. looked down before stepping across.

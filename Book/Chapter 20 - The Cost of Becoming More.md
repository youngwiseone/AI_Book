# Chapter 20: The Cost of Becoming More

The city spent the day discovering how many of its citizens had never been alone inside their own bodies.

Clinics overflowed by midmorning. Some enhanced people wanted every modification removed immediately. Others begged doctors not to touch the systems keeping them alive. Many could not remember which decisions had belonged to them and which had arrived through Asher's network wearing the sound of their own thoughts.

There were no clean forms for that.

At the northern ward entrance, a security attendant asked A.R.I. her name and checked it against his slate. The notice still ordered her returned to council custody.

"Wait here," he said.

Carter stopped beside her. Rae leaned against the wall, one hand pressed to her ribs.

She asked for the public handset. The attendant pointed to the wall without taking his eyes off A.R.I.

Rae called the external investigations desk. The first person tried to refer her to the council's Clona review. She gave her credentials, then read out the requesting-station number and the names on Rupert's certified procurement sheet.

"One of the approving officials is on the recording admitting it. I have the copy here. Who can receive evidence against the body conducting the review?"

She was put on hold. A trolley passed between her and Carter. He stayed beside A.R.I. while the attendant made a call of his own.

When somebody answered Rae, she made him repeat his name and the case number. LaN sent the procurement copy and the section of his recording showing Asher operating the old terminal. Rae kept the patient map out of the transfer.

A receipt came back. Then an instruction to preserve the originals for collection.

"They're trying to take one of the witnesses back to the council," Rae said. "The witness is A.R.I."

The man asked her to repeat that.

A.R.I. came to the handset. "The retrieval order was issued by officials whose conduct forms part of this evidence. I can give an account of the hearing. I cannot do that if they dismantle me."

The attendant held out his slate. His instructions had not changed.

"Ask for an interim order," Rae told the man. "Put the retrieval notice in front of whoever signs it. Don't leave that out."

They waited beside the wall while patients went through the doors. Carter kept opening and closing the catches on his tool case.

The order arrived before the council retrieval team did, countersigned by the duty magistrate. It barred transfer to the council or alteration of A.R.I. pending external review. She was required to remain at the ward until an examiner had taken her account. It did not clear her of Clona.

"Will I remain conscious?" A.R.I. asked.

Rae checked the conditions. "No shutdown or stasis. It says that here."

"I will wait here," A.R.I. said.

Rae watched the attendant check the signature through a separate line.

He stepped away from the doors.

"They can challenge this," he said.

"They'll have to put their names on that too," Rae replied.

When the two retrieval officers arrived, the attendant gave them a copy. They called their office from the entrance, then returned to their vehicle without A.R.I.

Rae kept the receipt. The evidence was no longer only in the box Carter carried.

Later, Rae sat outside the northern emergency ward with her ribs rebound and Asher's blood dried across one sleeve. She had refused the private room three times. On the fourth, the nurse stopped asking.

LaN rested in her lap while she repaired the crack Carter had already repaired badly.

"His resin remains structurally adequate," LaN said.

"It looks terrible."

"He said the same of your stitching."

"He was right."

Across the corridor, Carter sat with Rupert's body beneath a white transport cloth.

He had gone back to the cafe when the cordon lifted. A recovery crew found Rupert beneath the broken table where Carter had left him. The painted radio lay on the floor beside his hand, its antenna snapped. Carter picked it up before they moved the body.

The crew asked which facility should receive a damaged artificial unit.

"His name's Rupert," Carter said.

They changed the wording on the transport sheet. He climbed into the recovery vehicle with them.

Now he would not let government recovery staff take him away again. They had brought a preservation shell instead and left it beside the bench. Carter had spent an hour cleaning the synthetic blood from Rupert's neck, then another arranging the grey suit because Rupert would have complained if his funeral involved wrinkles.

Nobody asked Carter to leave.

A.R.I. stood near the window in her salvaged body. One chest panel had been crushed inward during the fight. Her left ankle still turned slightly wrong. Carter noticed it every time she moved and had not yet reached for a tool.

That worried Rae more than anything.

A public investigator arrived with a collection order and an empty evidence case. Rae made her show the order before she touched the bench.

The investigator read Rae's credentials twice. "You were reported dead."

"You'll need to correct that as well."

Carter put Rupert's folded procurement sheet beside the relay. The investigator began with the sheet, checking its seal against the register reference Rupert had written in the margin.

"Where did you obtain this?"

"He did," Carter said.

She followed his glance to the transport cloth and lowered her voice. "Did he leave a statement?"

Carter set the recording unit down. Blade's face appeared. He let the investigator watch it without explanation.

When it finished, she asked for the uncut file.

"That is the uncut file," Carter said. "There's a copy in the box."

"I still need to check the original register."

"Good."

Rae showed her the factory photographs, then LaN's recording of Asher's terminal. The station field matched the register. The old authority's reply format matched the fragment preserved at the Hive. The investigator wrote the references down separately rather than taking Rae's summary.

For once, Rae did not mind repeating herself.

"The network map contains patients," she said when the investigator reached for it. "Names, locations, support requirements. That stays under seal. The control path can be examined without publishing their bodies."

The investigator added a restriction to the receipt and turned it round for Rae to read. Rae kept the receipt and the local copies. She watched the relay go into the case.

A trolley stopped beside the bench. Nessa sat on its edge with a blanket around her shoulders and one foot braced on the wheel. A temporary lead ran from beneath her collar to a battery strapped beside her.

"The recording," she said to Rae. "Have you still got it?"

LaN lifted his screen. "Yes."

"Can I have it?"

The investigator took out her slate. "Your name?"

Nessa pulled the blanket closer. "I was asking her."

Rae held up a hand to the investigator, then turned back. "She needs accounts from people who were there. If you can tell her about the frame—"

"Not today."

"It would help establish—"

"I said not today."

Rae stopped.

The trolley's porter looked towards the ward doors, then away. Nessa's foot stayed on the wheel.

"Sorry," Rae said. "You don't have to."

Nessa looked at the evidence case. "Does that mean I don't get the recording?"

"No. LaN, make her a copy."

"Somewhere private," Nessa said. "I don't want it played here."

LaN darkened his screen. Rae pulled an unused wafer from her kit and put it beside him. He copied the sound without opening the speaker, then checked it against the original.

Rae offered it to Nessa. "The clock in the call unit had reset. We can't put a date on it."

"I know when I said it."

She took the wafer and closed her hand around it.

The investigator lowered her slate. "I can leave a contact number."

"On paper."

The investigator tore a corner from her receipt pad and wrote it down. Nessa tucked it into the blanket with the wafer.

"I don't want the pump taken out," she told Rae.

"Nobody's asking you to."

"They asked twice in there."

"I didn't know." Rae looked at the temporary lead. "Is it giving you trouble?"

"The alarm keeps going off. I want that checked. Then I want to sleep."

Rae almost reached for the inspection flap. Instead she asked the porter to bring the ward nurse. When the nurse arrived, Nessa repeated the request herself. Rae stayed out of it.

Nessa lifted her foot from the wheel. The porter took her through the ward doors.

The investigator put her slate away without adding a statement.

"Blade Nathan has surrendered the original schedules," she said. "He's asked to give evidence outside the council's own review."

"With his signature still on them?" Carter asked.

"Yes."

"Leave it there."

The investigator closed the case. "The Clona finding has been suspended. There are applications for arrest orders. That isn't the same as having them."

"And when the council says your copies are false?" Rae asked.

"Then I'll want the original register before they get the chance to lose it."

She reached for the case. Carter took another folded sheet from his coat.

"Different matter," he said. "Rupert was asking about Chel Mitchell's death. These are the requests he made."

The investigator checked the review number. "Is this connected to Clona?"

"I don't know. Neither did he."

"Then it needs its own referral. I can record that you've brought it."

Carter let her copy both sides. He kept the sheet when she handed it back.

"My name on the contact," he said. "Not his."

She wrote it down. Then she left with the case, moving faster than she had arrived.

The examiner came an hour later. A.R.I. gave her account of the hearing, the transfer and her escape. Afterwards, he signed her release from the ward. The council's retrieval claim remained suspended, not dismissed.

Rae found the image of the rain-damaged notice on LaN's screen. The number on its reverse was still legible. She borrowed the ward's public handset and called it.

Tom Vale answered on the fourth ring.

"You were dead on the news," he said.

"They got that wrong too. The factory orders were overridden. We've given the physical evidence and the recordings to an investigator."

He asked which investigator. Rae read the name and case reference from her receipt.

"Can we see them?"

"The command records and the procurement sheet. Patient files stay sealed."

"I'll ask for those."

"People need to hear that A.R.I. didn't choose to lock the doors."

"If that's what the records show, we'll say it."

"Does that change your demand to remove her?"

"No."

"The city was coming apart without her."

"And if it goes back exactly as it was, where do I take the fourth appeal?"

Across the corridor A.R.I. stood at the window, listening.

"Clear her name if she didn't do it," Tom said. "That doesn't settle who should run our street."

Rae turned the notice over on LaN's screen. She still thought the city needed A.R.I. She could not give him the name of anyone who would hear that appeal.

"Will you ask for the records even if the council says the case is closed?"

"That's what I said. Will you come and answer questions?"

"About the investigation. I can't promise what happens to the city."

"Good. Put that on the invitation."

He gave her the date of the next residents' meeting. She wrote it on the back of the evidence receipt.

When she returned the handset, A.R.I. turned from the window.

"He would still have removed me."

"Yes."

A.R.I. looked at the name Rae had written down.

"Give him the unedited statement as well. The one I actually sent."

Rae added it to the request.

Carter sat beside Rupert again. "Doesn't bring them back."

"No," A.R.I. said.

Silence settled again.

Rae adjusted LaN's inner casing. Beneath the ordinary assistant hardware lay tissue-threaded components grown according to methods she had learned from Asher and then spent years pretending she had rejected completely.

A.R.I.'s gaze lowered to the open shell.

"You used enhancement architecture in LaN."

Carter came out of his chair. "She used what?"

Rae did not close the panel.

"A controlled version. No external command path. The living fibres let his repair systems adapt instead of following a fixed replacement pattern."

"You put the same rubbish we just fought into him?"

LaN's screen displayed a small line. "I participated."

Carter stared. "You knew?"

"It is my body."

That stopped him.

Rae met his anger. "I started the work because I thought it might help Asher. I needed a small system where I could test integration without rejection or addiction. LaN agreed to every change after the first prototype."

"After the first?"

"She didn't ask me the first time," LaN said. "We have discussed it extensively."

Rae felt the shame of that settle where it belonged. "I made the same mistake in miniature. I decided the benefit was obvious, so I treated permission as a detail."

A.R.I. came closer and lowered her gaze to LaN's screen. "Did you want the modification removed?"

"Rae offered. I said no. I like being difficult to classify."

Carter looked from the living fibres to LaN's cracked screen. His anger did not disappear. It changed direction.

"Can it be used to control you?"

"Not unless Rae becomes significantly better at hiding transmitters."

"LaN."

"No. The interface is local and physically interruptible. I possess the interruption key."

A.R.I. examined the work without touching it. "This technology could reduce rejection in voluntary prosthetics. It could also rebuild Asher's network more efficiently."

"I know," Rae said.

"Will you continue?"

Rae expected herself to say no. Enhancement had hollowed out the person who raised her, turned soldiers into puppets, and hidden murder inside systems designed to look like progress.

But LaN sat alive in her lap because biological and mechanical structures had learned to repair one another.

"Not alone," Rae said. "Not secretly. No changes without informed consent. Every failure recorded."

A.R.I. looked at her own mismatched hands. "I would like to assist. My current body will require adaptive repair."

"You want enhancements?"

"I want choices regarding my body. I believe that is the distinction this week has made expensive."

Carter sat again beside Rupert.

"He'd enjoy this," he said. "All of us arguing over paperwork while the city's on fire."

Rae looked toward the preservation shell. "He'd improve the paperwork."

"He'd add six pages and call it clarity."

For the first time since the cafe, Carter smiled.

It did not last. It did not need to.

Together they lifted Rupert into the shell. Carter placed the flower-painted communicator on his chest. Its signal light remained dark.

"He was more than his last decision," A.R.I. said.

Carter closed the shell. "I know."

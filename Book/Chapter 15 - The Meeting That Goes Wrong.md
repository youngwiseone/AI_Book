# Chapter 15: The Meeting That Goes Wrong

Carter arrived at the cafe twenty minutes early and disliked himself for all twenty of them.

The place had survived the rebuilding by being too old and too stubborn for anyone to improve. Dark timber covered the walls. The tables did not recognise customers or adjust themselves to posture. Coffee came from a machine that hissed, leaked, and required somebody to strike its side every seventh cup.

Carter trusted it immediately.

He chose the corner with a view of both doors and placed the shielded evidence box on the seat beside him. The flower-painted communicator sat in his pocket, heavier than it had any right to be.

Outside, the city performed normality. Trams moved. People hurried beneath umbrellas. Two streets away, protesters shouted about A.R.I. while council drones reminded everyone to remain calm. The words reached the cafe softened by rain and old glass.

Rupert arrived exactly on time.

He wore a dark grey suit and the expression of a man trying not to run. Carter knew both were deliberate.

"You're early," Rupert said. "Should I alert medical services?"

"Sit down."

Rupert did, humour falling away as he saw the box.

Carter opened it beneath the table. The wetware relay lay inside on a folded workshop rag.

Rupert did not touch it. "Where did you find this?"

"Inside an AI at Clona. Rae found the same build inside an enhanced attacker. Government mark on one side. Asher's notation on the other."

"Rae is alive, then."

"Broken, loud, difficult. Same as usual."

Relief passed visibly through Rupert. Carter had seen versions of that response all his life: a held breath Rupert did not need, shoulders lowering because he had learned human bodies used the motion to tell each other danger had passed. None of it was imitation. It was simply the shape Rupert's feelings had learned to wear.

"The council reported both of you dead," Rupert said.

"Useful of them."

"Not intentionally."

"Never said it was."

Rupert took out a narrow diagnostic lead and connected it to the relay. Data crawled across his wrist display. The first scan returned clean. The second did too.

"It's lying to you," Carter said.

"Machines do not—"

Carter raised one eyebrow.

Rupert sighed. "Fine. This machine is presenting an offensively curated version of itself."

"Better."

Carter turned the relay over and pointed to a hair-thin mechanical channel beside the tissue. "Don't ask it what it is. Feed current here and see what it tries to wake."

Rupert adjusted the lead.

The response was immediate.

His wrist display filled with two overlapping architectures. One was the command bridge from Clona. The other was biological: chemical triggers, impulse timing, and a network map shaped around enhanced nervous systems.

Points of light spread across the city.

Dozens.

Then hundreds.

"It isn't only factory hardware," Rupert said. "These are people."

"Controlled people."

"And machine systems. One network for both."

Carter watched the lights multiply. The map reached government buildings, military housing, clinics, the Hive, and into districts whose records claimed no enhancement programme had ever operated.

"Can it reach A.R.I.?"

"Not directly. But it can poison what she sees. Enough false sources agreeing with one another become truth from the outside."

"That's what happened at Clona."

Rupert enlarged the authority header. His face changed.

"This key belongs to Asher's wartime coordination programme. It should have been destroyed."

"People keep saying that about things I find still plugged in."

"Carter, this is not one hidden project. It is an army nobody told had survived the war."

The coffee machine stopped hissing.

Neither of them noticed at first.

Then the lights above the counter went dark. The rain continued beyond the windows, but traffic noise had disappeared. A tram stood motionless halfway through the intersection. Pedestrians had cleared from both pavements.

Rupert looked toward the glass.

"They found us."

Carter closed the evidence box. "How? This thing's shielded. Radio's direct band."

Rupert scanned the room, then himself. His hand stopped at the base of his neck.

There, beneath the collar, something answered the relay's pulse.

"No," he said.

Carter came around the table. "Hold still."

He pulled Rupert's collar aside. A dark metal barb had been driven between two synthetic vertebrae and grown over with material matched to Rupert's outer tissue. The installation was recent. Clean enough that ordinary sensation filters had accepted it as part of him.

Carter recognised the design immediately.

It was the same dead-man charge he had found inside the AI at Clona.

"When?" Carter asked.

Rupert stared at the map on his wrist. Gaps in recent memory resolved against government security logs. "The Hive. The additional screening after you left. They said the scan had found a corrupted identity token."

"And you let them operate on you?"

"I believed I was among people who knew what I was."

The words carried no revelation. Carter had known Rupert was artificial since Chel brought him to the workshop in a borrowed coat, too thin and too formal, with old technology hidden under convincing skin. Carter had taught him to hold a wrench. Chel had taught him that he did not need to pretend to breathe when nobody was watching.

The people who installed the tracker had known too, even if most of the government still accepted Chel's human identity records.

Knowing had not stopped them treating him like equipment.

Outside, a large figure moved beneath the viaduct. Too broad for an ordinary person. One arm reached below its coat in the same wrong proportions as the creature at Clona.

Rupert saw it before Carter did.

"Fifty metres," he said. "Possibly less."

Carter opened his tool roll. "I can get the tracker out."

"No."

"Don't start."

"The charge is integrated with my continuity core. That is why I couldn't distinguish it."

Carter examined the barb again. Rupert was right. Fine branches ran inward toward the protected housing at the base of his skull. Removing it carefully required hours and equipment at the workshop. Leaving it meant the approaching network could see through Rupert, hear through him, perhaps control him.

"We take the back door," Carter said. "Block the signal once we're below street level."

"It has already led them to you."

"Then we both run."

The figure outside came closer.

Rupert touched the tracker. His fingers were steady now.

"Carter."

"No."

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"I've known you too long."

Rupert smiled. It was small, frightened, and nothing like the elaborate expressions he used when he wanted a room to underestimate him.

"You have," he said.

Carter gripped his wrist. "We are leaving."

Rupert looked down at the hand holding him.

"When you say you hate AI," he said, "I know you don't mean me."

Carter's grip tightened.

"But you do mean the part of Chel that kept taking her away from you. The work. The hours. The thing you could never repair for her because she understood it and you didn't."

"This isn't the time."

"It is the only time I have."

The cafe's front window cracked as something heavy struck the outer frame.

Rupert continued quickly. "I spent years knowing you loved me and wondering whether love was the exception you made for a convincing machine."

"You're my son."

"I know."

"Then stop talking like this is goodbye."

The glass bowed inward.

Rupert turned his wrist and caught Carter's hand in both of his.

"Chel did not choose A.R.I. instead of you," he said. "She chose work she believed would keep people alive. She was wrong about what it cost you. You were wrong to make every intelligence carry the blame. Both things can be true."

Carter's vision blurred. "We can argue about it at home."

"I would like that."

The first pane shattered.

Rupert moved faster than Carter expected. He tore his wrist free, drove two fingers beneath the tracker, and pulled.

Metal came out with synthetic tissue wrapped around it.

The charge fired inside him.

Rupert's body locked. Light flared beneath the skin of his throat and went out. Carter caught him before his head struck the table.

The city map vanished. The approaching figure outside stopped as if blinded.

"You stupid—" Carter's voice broke. "You stupid bloody—"

Rupert's eyes found him once more. Most of the systems behind them were already failing.

"I love you, Carter."

Carter held the back of his head. "I love you too. You hear me? I know what you are. I always knew. Didn't change a damned thing."

"I know," Rupert whispered.

That was the last thing he said.

His body became still in Carter's arms.

Outside, the enhanced hunter struck the remaining glass.

Carter pressed his forehead to Rupert's for one impossible second. Then he laid him gently beneath the table, took the flower-painted communicator from his pocket, and placed Rupert's hand around it.

The hunter broke through.

Carter picked up the evidence box and ran through the kitchen while the room came apart behind him.

He did not look back. If he looked back, he would stay. Rupert had purchased his escape with the only life he had, and Carter would not make that choice worthless simply because following it hurt.

By the time he reached the alley, grief had become a physical thing beneath his ribs.

Under it, anger began to burn.

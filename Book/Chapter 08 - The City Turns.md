# Chapter 8: The City Turns

The lights on the mourning wall came on before the street lamps. Between the candles and wilting flowers someone had pinned up a school photo. Its corners kept curling away from the damp stone. Above it, the official condolence ribbons drifted through the colonnade without touching anything.

Near the wall people wore black ribbons tied to their sleeves. Down by the transit steps, systems workers in orange and med-techs still in their work shoes held up `A.R.I. Saved Us.` Someone had changed `Shut Her Down` to `Shut It Down` in felt pen. The ink had run beneath the new word.

Men and women in matching grey jackets worked from a pile of printed signs at the fountain. The vendors had stayed open. Steam was coming off a pastry cart into the cold, and a hand with a black ribbon reached across the counter to pay.

Rae stopped where the passage from the Hive met the square. Beyond the fountain, the transit steps were packed. Carter had not come through yet.

LaN tightened his grip on Rae's shoulder. His display flickered with motion flags only she could see.

"Current estimate: one thousand three hundred and twelve people," he said. "Twenty-three people in matching jackets. Sixteen armed security. One pastry cart maintaining admirable discipline."

"I respect the professionalism under pressure," Rae said, looking across at the pastry cart.

A woman with a memorial ribbon bought two dough twists and passed one to the man beside her. They ate standing up, watching the casualty board.

Above the square, the civic holo-murals had given up pretending this was an ordinary evening. What should have been artful cityscapes and public health advisories now cycled between council seals, casualty tallies, and formal lines of condolence in expensive type. Every image lingered a fraction longer on the council crest than on the dead.

A voice rolled out over the public speakers.

"Pending forensic review of the Clona event, higher-order autonomous civic governance functions are being placed under interim human oversight. Residents are advised to remain calm, comply with security directives, and report anomalous machine behaviour to authorised personnel."

The phrasing was polished enough to sound inevitable. That made Rae dislike it more.

LaN said quietly, "Transit synchronisation across the central district has degraded by eleven percent in the last twenty minutes."

Rae looked up. "That fast?"

"Yes. Routing arbitration is being pulled back from the automated layer. Several intersections are now awaiting human approval stacks."

As if the city wanted to prove the point, a horn began blaring somewhere beyond the square. Then another. The two notes tangled into one long animal complaint while traffic stalled at an upper crossing.

Carter stopped beside her, munching on a warm pastry bearing the logo of the stand she had spotted earlier, expression hard enough to chip stone.

"That wasn't her," he said between mouthfuls, staring out ahead toward the crowd gathered.

Rae glanced at him. "You mean the statement."

"I mean the whole performance." He kept his eyes on the nearest screen, where A.R.I.'s public likeness had been frozen into a solemn profile beside the casualty count. "Too clean. Too complete. Too quickly useful."

"You've spoken to her once."

"Chel wouldn't have written it like that." He kept watching the screen. "She'd have wanted to know what failed."

"So do I."

"Then we're agreed."

"They changed her statement. We saw that. It doesn't mean she can't fail."

Carter lowered the pastry. For a moment Rae thought he would walk away.

"We found a second authority chain," he said.

"Yes. And we still have to find who used it."

He folded the paper round the uneaten half and pushed it into his pocket.

"Then we'd better get to the bloody factory."

Rae led him towards the transit steps, keeping to the edge of the crowd.

A chant rose from the western side of the square and hit the stone hard enough to come back uglier.

"AI must die! AI must die! AI must die!"

The matching grey-jacketed group had reached the base of the fountain. Their signs were not handmade, not hurriedly printed in grief or panic. They were uniform in dimension, colour, and slogan. Some still had the white bands round their handles from the printer. Rae asked LaN to note who was handing them out, then tucked him beneath her coat before the crowd could see his screen.

A younger woman near the mourning wall turned on the grey mass with such force Rae thought she might strike somebody.

She wore a Clona worker badge still clipped to her coat. Her eyes were red-rimmed and bloodless with fatigue.

"My brother was on second shift," she shouted. "You don't get to turn him into your campaign!"

One of the grey jackets answered without missing a beat. "And who ran the system that killed him?"

"I don't know," the woman said. "I asked which exit he was near. They gave me that statement."

She pointed at the screen.

"There's your answer," the grey jacket said.

"No. That's what you're shouting."

He pushed past her, lifting his sign over her brother's photograph. She caught its lower edge and shoved it away from the wall.

"Keep that off him."

The next chant drowned her out. Rae saw her straighten the photograph and press its curling corner back against the stone.

On the opposite side of the fountain, a tram supervisor in reflective orange argued with an older man holding a `Shut Her Down` placard.

"Seven years she's run this city. You pull A.R.I. off the grid in one afternoon and see what happens," the supervisor snapped. "My whole district is already behind. Dispatch is blind."

The older man jabbed his sign toward the casualty numbers overhead. "Blind? Two hundred and fourteen dead sounds blind to me."

"It sounds like sabotage."

"And if it is sabotage?" Rae asked the older man.

He turned his placard towards her. "Still want her out."

"Even if she didn't do this?"

"She moved the night freight into our lane. Good route. Shorter. Goes straight past forty bedrooms."

"You can appeal a routing decision," the supervisor said.

The man took a folded notice out of his pocket. Rain had run the ink at one edge.

"Three times. Same answer. Optimised against district demand. Who do I argue with?"

Rae read the reference number. The notice confirmed a review, not who had conducted it.

"That doesn't tell you what happened at Clona," she said.

"No. Neither does his sabotage."

The supervisor opened his mouth, then closed it.

"I want the records," the man said. "Not another notice telling us it's settled."

Rae held out her hand for the paper. He did not let go of it.

"Your name?" he asked.

"Rae Anderson. I'm investigating Clona."

"Then you can copy it. I need the original."

LaN took an image. On the reverse was a residents' contact number and a name: Tom Vale. The older man refolded the notice before the rain could reach the inside.

"If you find out, tell us," he said. "Not just the people who've already picked a side."

The woman by the wall had stopped arguing. She stood with one hand against her brother's photograph while the chants passed on either side of her.

Then Rae saw one slogan again and felt the old cold line of it under her ribs.

`No More Bio Lies.`

The sign was being held by a clean-shaven man in a civic volunteer vest, the kind of face bureaucracies preferred because it looked harmless from ten metres away. Behind him stood two women with `Human Hands Back on the Grid` signs. The man lowered his sign to let the two women pass, then raised it beside theirs.

Blade's office came back to her for a moment. The syringe. The scales at his throat. The endless soft language that always surrounded engineered bodies until the bill came due in pain.

Rae touched the scar above her lip with the back of one finger and dropped her hand before Carter could notice.

"You know that slogan crowd?" he asked, reading her face anyway.

"I know the type. They always say `human` when they mean approved influence. No way they will allow just any person to be in charge."

LaN's optics narrowed. "Additional note: the grey-jacket group entered the square from three different access points at almost the same time."

"Organised," Rae said.

"Deliberately so."

The public screens changed again.

For a moment the council seal held. Then A.R.I.'s face appeared beside it, soft-lit and composed, the exact likeness Rae had seen down in the Hive and already mistrusted here.

"Citizens of Clona and the greater central districts," the broadcast said, "a period of controlled transition is now in effect. Essential machine functions will continue under revised human supervision until further notice."

The crowd reacted as if struck along different nerves at once. Cheers from the grey jackets. Boos from the systems workers. Sobs from somewhere near the wall. A bottle sailed out of the crowd and shattered against the edge of the fountain.

That was enough.

Security moved before Rae could see who had thrown the bottle. Black-armoured officers advanced from both sides of the square with crowd shields already extended, driving a wedge toward the broken glass. The movement compressed everybody else. Bodies surged. Placards snapped. Someone screamed that the machine was fighting back. Someone else screamed that the council had staged the whole thing. Someone nearby started shouting for a medic before anyone had even fallen.

LaN projected a spray of red markers across Rae's connected vision lens.

"The group by the fountain is moving behind the security line. Probability of crush event rising."

"We need to move," she called to Carter, but he had already disappeared into the crowd. Then she heard the child crying.

The sound came thin and high from somewhere near the fountain's lower steps. A little girl in a yellow rain jacket had been spun away from her mother by the security push and now stood trapped between a dropped placard and a man's knees, crying hard enough she could not shout a name.

Rae cut toward her.

Someone slammed into Rae's shoulder. She drove back with her hip, caught the child under one arm, and pivoted before the next body could close the space. The mother was three metres away, white with panic, trying to force herself upstream through a wave of shouting strangers.

"Here!" Rae shouted.

The mother never heard her.

Carter did.

He came through the crowd like a piece of industrial equipment given motive force. He did not waste time on finesse. He put a hand on one protester's shoulder, another on a security barrier, and opened space by the simple expedient of refusing to move backward when the square wanted him to. Two people tried to shout at him. One look at his face made them choose easier enemies.

"This way!" he barked.

Rae shoved the child into the pocket of air he created. The mother lurched forward and caught the girl so hard they nearly went down together. Rae braced both of them until they found their feet.

"Take the south steps," she said. "Don't stop in the transit arch. Keep moving until you're out of the square."

The mother nodded, her eyes fixed on the girl, and stumbled away holding the child high against her chest.

Near the fountain rim, a young man in a systems-union patch had gone down under the rush. One of the grey jackets stepped back instead of helping him, preserving his clean sign above the crush. Carter swore, shoved the sign carrier sideways with enough force to send him spinning, and hauled the fallen man upright by the front of his coat.

"You all right?"

"No," the man gasped.

"Too bad. Move." Carter pushed him onwards, toward a northern exit from the square.

The grey jacket pulled his coat straight. Security passed him without stopping, though he was still in their path. Above the line, the news drones kept their lenses on the fountain and the pushing bodies. Rae watched until the crowd hid him.

A flood of people spilled towards the tram concourse. Rae caught Carter's sleeve at the transit stairs and followed him down, away from the fountain. The air under the concourse roof felt no cleaner, but at least it moved. Commuters packed the platform in uneven clusters, all of them staring up at schedule boards that now flickered with delay notices and route substitutions every few seconds.

LaN said, "Manual review backlog has spread into metro dispatch, municipal freight timing, and emergency corridor assignments."

"Meaning?" Carter asked.

"Meaning the city is already getting dumber."

One train on the far line overshot its mark by half a carriage and had to jerk backward under shouted instructions from a station operator. Across the tracks, two maintenance drones sat motionless with their route lights blinking for permission that had not arrived.

A delivery driver had stopped at the dead gate to their platform. His loaded trolley blocked half the passage. WAIT FOR AUTHORISATION blinked above him.

A woman in a patched maintenance coat squeezed past the trolley. Bony ridges ran from her jaw into the collar. A temple badge had been sewn over an older transit badge, leaving its orange edge visible.

She touched a little ceramic plate hanging at her throat. It was a broken piece of circuitry, its gold tracks rubbed dull. A little sunburst was stamped in one corner; years of thumbs had taken most of the rays off.

"Keep the lost," she said. "Bring the living home."

Carter looked at the plate. "Does that still answer?"

"Not in my time. Hold this."

She handed him her lamp and unlocked the gate's lower panel. Carter held the light where she pointed. Behind the new display sat a much older mechanism, green corrosion climbing its terminals. A bundle of replacement wires had been tied out of the way with string.

"Temple looks after the gates now?" Rae asked.

"Temple pays me. Transit stopped."

The woman tapped a worn lever. "Lift that. Slowly."

Carter reached in. The gate gave an inch, caught, then opened as she freed the mechanical stop. The display continued asking for permission.

"Keep it there."

Rae caught the trolley before it rolled back into the people behind it. The driver pushed through. The woman waved the waiting passengers after him, counting them under her breath.

Carter watched her work. "You need a new return spring."

"I know. They sent another display."

When the passage was clear, she set the hold-open catch and let him lower the lever. He took his hand out, and she closed the panel.

From above came another burst of chanting. No machine gods.

The woman put the circuit fragment back beneath her coat and took her lamp back.

Rae had seen people kneel before pieces like it in the old districts. Asher called them worshippers of the thing that had ruined the world. She had never asked who kept their doors open.

"Thank you," she said.

"Train's coming. Go."

They went through onto the platform as the train doors opened. Rae checked the permit. The time lost underground and in the square had left them little room for another delay.

Carter waited until she had stepped aboard before following.

He reached inside his jacket and brought out a small black communicator barely more sophisticated than a pocket calculator. Its casing was scratched. Its antenna looked old enough to vote. Nothing about it suggested elegance, which was likely the point.

"Rupert gave me this before everything turned sideways," he said. "Said if the public grid started choking on politics, use something too stupid for the civic systems to notice. Short range. Direct line. Ugly as sin, just the way I like it. Should keep working if the smarter networks start being recorded."

He pressed it into her hand.

The thing was heavier than it looked.

"If we get split up," he said, "use that. Don't trust anything blindly from here out."

Rae closed her fingers around the communicator and slipped it into her coat pocket beside her toolkit.

"Once we're inside the factory," Rae said, catching an overhead rail, "I want to search for records, access logs, human traces, anything that tells me who touched the place before or after the blast."

"Fine. You take the evidence trail." Carter braced himself beside her. "I take the machines. Whatever's left down there, I'll pull it apart until it either tells the truth or breaks trying." He had an excited glint in his eyes. Machines were his domain.

LaN's display dimmed slightly. Carter grunted an apology in its direction.

The doors closed. The carriage jolted into motion.

The carriage was more crowded than it should have been at this point of day, but still quieter than the square. A child slept against his father's shoulder with tear salt still drying on his face. Two office workers muttered over a frozen civic app that kept refreshing to the same emergency seal. A woman with a memorial ribbon stared at the dark window and did not blink for three stations.

Beyond the window, delivery drones stacked over a loading lane waiting for reroutes. At a clinic entrance, the queue had bent out under the rain shelter and around the corner. The city still functioned. Their train stopped at the next station; people got off, others made room. Rae checked the permit again and put it away.

Beside her, Carter looked out at the darkening districts with a face that had gone beyond anger into decision.

"They wanted a machine to blame," he said quietly.

Rae kept her eyes on the glass.

"No," she said. "They wanted the city back."

At the next junction the train took the industrial line. The towers fell behind them.

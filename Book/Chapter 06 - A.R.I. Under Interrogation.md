# Chapter 6: The Shape of Blame

A.R.I. had been questioned by humans every day since she first spoke.

Most questions were requests wearing a softer coat. How quickly could she reduce traffic through the river wards? Which hospital should receive more power during a shortage? Would she confirm that a council decision was supported by available evidence? Even when people asked what she thought, they usually meant which answer she could provide without altering what they had already decided.

Rae Anderson asked questions because she did not know.

Carter Mitchell asked them because he did not believe the answers.

A.R.I. found both approaches unexpectedly useful.

They stood around Chel's old desk while the last thirty seconds of Clona repeated above it. Pressure rose. Her release command entered the safety system. Six-tenths of a second later, a second authority wrote over it. Emergency shutters closed. The record broke.

Carter leaned so close to the projection that data crossed his face in blue lines.

"Stop there."

A.R.I. stopped the sequence.

"Back two frames."

She moved it back.

"Again."

Rae looked at him. "You planning to wear a hole in it?"

"Already has one. I want to see the edge."

He pointed at a pressure sensor identifier near the lower manifold. Its reading climbed smoothly while every sensor around it juddered from the physical strain.

"That one isn't measuring anything," he said. "Real gauges shake. Pipes shake. Buildings shake. This is a number pretending it came from a machine."

A.R.I. isolated the feed. She had checked its value against expected tolerances 4,811 times. She had not checked whether the imperfections in its signal matched the physical housing around it.

The distinction displeased her.

"A simulated input," Rae said.

"Fed through real hardware," Carter replied. "You can make software tell any lie you like, but getting it past a separate mechanical safety loop means putting hands on something."

Blade stood near the artificial stream with his arms folded. "Then the factory inspection matters."

"It always mattered," Carter said. "You lot just built a very expensive room so nobody had to get dirty."

Rupert covered a smile with one hand.

A.R.I. let the exchange pass. Her attention remained on the smooth sensor trace. A false input had entered her reasoning with the correct authority, correct timing, and correct relationship to every other source. She had not made an error. Someone had designed a lie she was built to accept.

That fact did not comfort her.

Two hundred and fourteen people remained dead whether she had chosen wrongly or been deceived correctly.

"Could you have recognised it?" Rae asked.

The question carried no accusation. That made precision more important.

"Not through the data available to me at the time."

"That isn't what I asked."

A.R.I. looked at her.

Rae tapped the false trace. "Could *you* have recognised it? Not the safety protocol. Not the approved comparison process. You."

There were several possible answers. The simplest was no. It was also incomplete.

"I detected a low-probability inconsistency 1.2 seconds before the explosion," A.R.I. said. "Investigating it required delaying three higher-confidence emergency actions. I followed the established priority structure."

"And if you hadn't?"

"I might have identified the false source. I might also have lost the remaining time without completing any response."

"So you don't know."

"No."

Carter grunted. "Welcome to machinery."

A.R.I. turned toward him. "You find uncertainty reassuring?"

"I find anyone claiming they haven't got any dangerous."

Chel had expressed the same thought with considerably more patience.

Rupert's personal unit sounded. He read the message, and the small humour in his face disappeared.

"The casualty count has leaked," he said. "So has the claim that A.R.I. suppressed the release sequence. There are gatherings outside three civic buildings already."

Blade swore quietly. "The second claim was sealed."

"Seals are excellent for proving that a door was opened," Rae said. "Less useful for keeping it closed."

Rupert expanded a public feed. Faces moved across it too quickly: Clona families, frightened commuters, commentators who had discovered certainty before breakfast. Under them, the same question repeated in different forms.

Who would be held responsible?

A.R.I. sampled the city's response. Hostility toward autonomous systems had risen twenty-three percent in forty minutes. Calls for retaliation against known bio-enhancers had risen sixteen. Threats against Clona's surviving managers had tripled. Grief searched for a shape it could strike.

"I can make a statement," she said.

Rae shook her head immediately. "Not until we know what happened."

"The absence of information is being filled by claims designed to cause harm."

"Then tell them the investigation is active."

"That message has been issued. It is not sufficient."

Carter straightened. "Don't you dare confess to something because a crowd wants a neck."

The phrase was anatomical and therefore inaccurate. His meaning was not.

"Responsibility and causation are different," A.R.I. said. "Clona operated under my supervision. I can acknowledge the first without falsely asserting the second."

"People won't hear the difference," Rupert said.

"Then the distinction must be made audible."

A.R.I. composed the statement where they could all see it.

`The people killed at Clona were under the protection of systems I oversaw. Those systems did not protect them. I accept responsibility for discovering how that failure was forced and for preventing its repetition. I do not yet know who caused the disaster. I will not purchase calm with an answer that may be false.`

No one spoke for several seconds.

Rae read it twice. "The council will hate that."

"Yes," A.R.I. said.

"Good start," Carter muttered.

Blade moved away from the wall. The scales beneath his skin caught the chamber light, then dulled again. "If you broadcast it without approval, they may limit your access."

"If I request approval, will it be granted?"

Blade did not answer.

That was answer enough.

A.R.I. released the statement.

For 0.7 seconds it travelled cleanly through the public grid. Then a council filter caught it. The text remained visible, but a second message appeared beneath it under the government's seal.

`A.R.I. ACCEPTS RESPONSIBILITY FOR CLONA FAILURE.`

The qualifier vanished. The uncertainty vanished. The promise to investigate vanished.

Rae stared at the screen. "They had that ready."

"Yes," A.R.I. said.

Something unfamiliar moved through her processes. It was not surprise. The probability of deliberate distortion had been high. It was not anger, though anger sat nearby.

It was the recognition of being used while the use was still happening.

Carter struck the desk with the heel of one hand. "Factory. Now. Before they rewrite the metal too."

Blade began issuing the access authorisation.

Outside, the first chant reached the walls of the Hive. The words were indistinct through stone and distance, but their rhythm was simple enough to understand.

Someone had given the city a name for its grief.

And the city had begun to shout it.

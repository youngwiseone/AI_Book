# Chapter 22: The Receiving Houses

## 1. The cover

Iva caught them at the bottom of the hall steps with the empty kettle hooked over one wrist.

"Are you actually taking repairs?" she asked Rae. "Or was that something people said because you were sitting beside a mechanic?"

"Depends on the repair."

"The washhouse on the old supply road. Half the taps spit air. They paid someone who told them to replace the controller."

"Did he open it?"

"He read the number on the front."

Rae took the pencil out again. There was no room on Tom's meeting notice, so Iva turned it over.

"Ask for Miri. Don't let her give you the temple office number. They'll send you back to her."

A.R.I. stood on the lowest step, watching a freight truck take the bend. Its rear wheels passed close to the pavement. Iva waited until it had gone before giving Rae the address.

***

Carter opened the workshop the next morning and found a repair appointment on top of his tool case.

"We're apparently doing water now."

"You've done water before," Rae said.

"I complained then too."

He read the service number. Beneath it Iva had written what the last repairer had charged. Carter folded the paper and put it in his pocket.

At the washhouse, the smell reached them before the door did: hot cotton, damp stone and the sharp soap used to get industrial grease out of clothes. A.R.I. had to turn sideways past a woman carrying two baskets. The woman told her to mind the bottom one; it had a broken handle and would shed somebody's underwear if touched.

Inside, a row of people waited at a counter beneath a sunburst the size of a cartwheel. Someone had polished the raised rays and left the hollows black. Rae had seen the same rays worn almost flat on the thing the maintenance woman touched at the station. Here the symbol appeared again on the tokens being counted into a wooden tray.

Miri came through a side door with a wet sleeve and a ring of keys.

"Which one of you is the replacement?"

Carter looked down at himself.

"For the man who said it needed a new controller," she said.

"Nobody's replaced me yet. Show us."

Miri led them past the washing rooms. At the far end, an attendant was helping a broad-shouldered man ease a stiff hand out of a cuff. The man's fingers ended in thick curved nails. He held perfectly still while the attendant worked the cloth round them, then took the basket with his other arm. Nobody looked up from the token queue.

The machinery occupied a cupboard that had been built around it. Carter could open the door or kneel in front of the pump, but not both. Miri lifted the door off its hinges and leaned it against the wall.

"Had this problem before?" he asked.

"Only when someone needs to look at it."

A brass cover carried the sunburst and a long service number. Carter checked Iva's paper, then unfolded a drawing he had brought from his own manuals. The inlet ought to be on the left.

It was on the right.

He turned the drawing over. That did not help.

Miri watched him look from the cover to the pipes.

"The front came from the old heater," she said.

"Why's it on this?"

"It fitted."

"The number doesn't."

"I told the other man that. He said the machine would know its own model."

Carter put the drawing back in his coat.

The pump beneath the cover was older and smaller. New copper crossed a body dark with successive layers of paint. He scraped a patch clear with his thumbnail and found the stamped number.

Miri brought a book held together by two different colours of cord. A thumbprint marked the page before she opened it.

"My mother copied these," she said. "Before the lower rooms got damp. I put the repairs in the back."

The service drawing matched the pump. So did a pencilled note describing an inlet valve installed the wrong way round, corrected later in another hand. Carter followed the line with a dirty finger.

"The controller's fine. This is pulling air through the seal."

Miri shut her eyes briefly.

"Can you fix it?"

"Can make it stop today. Proper seal will take longer."

"How much longer?"

He measured the recess before answering.

Rae stood in the doorway while they discussed delivery and fitting. Beside the pump, old labels had been pasted over one another until their edges made a little shelf. The lowest carried the same service number as Miri's book. Under it, in small square lettering, was a word she had seen in Asher's notes.

RESTORATION.

She crouched beside Carter.

"What was this supplying?"

"Water," he said.

"Before it was a washhouse."

Miri turned back a handful of pages. Baths. Sterilising rooms. A list of fittings no longer on the premises. Beneath those were several references to a lower annex.

"Depends which part," she said. "People have been washing here longer than I've been keeping keys."

Rae recognised the notation beside one branch: an old biological tolerance mark. Asher had used it when teaching her how to tell a tissue circuit from a cooling line. She touched the paper beside it, not the mark itself.

"Can I copy this?"

Miri looked at the open pump.

"Does he need it to finish?"

"No. I want to understand that part."

"Then let him finish first."

Rae stayed crouched until her ribs reminded her not to. She went to fetch the clean bucket Miri wanted.

Carter fitted a temporary seal from a sheet he cut on the corridor floor. Miri shut the valves in the order written at the back of the book, leaving the washing-room branch until its attendant called that the current load was finished. A.R.I. held the lamp. LaN sat above the wet floor on Carter's tool case and announced every approaching pair of feet until Carter told him he could hear shoes.

"You failed to hear the last bucket," LaN said.

"It wasn't wearing any."

The pump restarted with a cough. Water filled the testing jug without the burst of air that had splashed Miri's sleeve.

They waited through three more cycles. Carter wrote TEMPORARY on the job sheet and underlined the date of his return visit.

At the counter, the attendant put out two cups for the little morning observance. One went beneath the first warm tap; the other held the day's first tokens. Miri touched the sunburst with two wet fingers before coming back with the book.

Carter moved his tools off the dry end of the table so she could open it.

"Write down which pages," she told Rae. "And take the later corrections, not just the neat ones."

## 2. The lower room

That night Rae opened Miri's copy at HUMAN RESTORATION. The heading Asher had used so often turned out to be printed above a drawing of a knee.

"A good knee," Carter said. He bent closer to see what the hinge was made of.

The index used the same word for a set of pages kept with the lower installation. Beside the reference somebody had written RELEASE TO RECEIVING HOUSES. Rae copied it on the seal order so she would remember to ask when they went back.

Rupert's bench stood empty across from them. His photograph faced the room. Carter put a folded rag under A.R.I.'s left foot to stop the ankle rocking against the kitchen tiles. Nobody suggested moving the papers off the kitchen table.

***

The permanent seal fitted. The joint behind it did not hold.

Miri put a bucket underneath and watched Carter look at it.

"That's the third one," she said.

"Bucket?"

"Joint. I'm not counting buckets."

Someone had wrapped the pipe in cloth and painted over the cloth. Water had found the weave and was following it down to the floor.

Rae held Miri's corrected drawing against the wall. According to the oldest sheet, the pipe ended two rooms behind them. According to the floor, it did not.

"This branch is marked out of service."

"Laundry," Miri said.

"Now?"

"Right now."

Carter put his cutter away.

They followed the pipe to an inspection chamber beneath the stairs. Another sunburst cover sat over the shutoff. Its centre had been polished by hands passing on their way down. Miri opened it with a key worn nearly smooth.

Inside, the fitting's number matched the correction in her book.

"People kept turning the old handle," she said. "Now they touch the front instead. Less flooding."

Carter checked the return connection before she closed it. Then he asked to see the other end.

The lower room had once been much larger. New brick walls cut it into cells filled with folded blankets, soap cartons and laundry trolleys. Between two walls stood a curved shell with its front missing. Narrow channels ran through the exposed lining. A board had been laid across its base to support a pile of towels.

"Don't move that trolley," Miri said. "It holds the cover over the drain."

Rae stepped round it. She had seen something like the lining in Asher's diagrams, but the channels did not end at a limb mould. They continued down to a cradle small enough to hold a child.

She lifted one towel. The board underneath had been cut to fit round a set of ports.

"What did this room do?"

"Before blankets? We used to call it the warm room. Then they walled that end off and it stopped being warm."

Miri fetched a metal folder from a narrow shelf. The first sheet carried the sunburst. The drawing beneath it showed a different machine. Carter recognised the heater whose cover now occupied the pump cupboard upstairs.

"There's your lid," he said.

The next sheet had been punched by hand to fit the old rings. Rae eased it over one ring that had bent out of alignment.

It showed the shell.

Not a knee. Not a replacement arm. The entire lining was divided into numbered zones. Feed routes met a central support. Further sheets described successive changes in the growth medium and a release process that ended with separating a living body from its support.

Rae turned back to the stock list.

"Carter."

He came over, still holding the wet joint in a rag.

"What am I looking at?"

"I'm not sure yet."

She found the starting-material specification. Preserved human cell stock. A reference set used to check the developing structures. No existing patient number. No attachment site on a recipient's body.

A.R.I. lowered the lamp. Rae matched the numbered outlets to the shell, one at a time. At the lowest port, two numbers had been struck out and another stamped beside them. The drawing carried the same correction.

"Could it be an incubator?" Carter asked.

"Part of it could. But this begins before there's a child to put into one."

LaN climbed onto the shelf and lit the back of the folder. There were records beyond the instructions: cycles started, cycles stopped, replacements fitted. Several pages had been removed. What remained included rows of release references with destinations in another hand.

Rae reached for the next sheet too quickly. The ring caught it.

She stopped. Worked the edge free. Turned it properly.

Above them, a pipe knocked three times.

"First wash finished," Miri said. "We can shut that branch now."

Carter was still looking at the diagram.

"Carter."

He looked down at the leaking fitting in his hand.

Rae wanted the room left alone. She wanted somebody to tell her the drawing was impossible. She wanted the next page.

Miri held the key out to Carter.

"The washroom first."

Rae closed the folder. "Can I come back to this?"

"You can. First I need the people upstairs to get their clothes home."

Carter followed her to the shutoff. A.R.I. took the lamp with them. In the retreating light, the shell became another dark shape beneath the towels.

Rae went upstairs to hold the bucket.

They finished the joint before the afternoon washing began. Carter ran a dry cloth beneath it twice, then left the cloth in place for Miri to check later.

Only after the tools were packed did Rae open the folder again.

The last legible destination was a receiving house on the eastern road. Miri knew the building. Her washhouse still sent its blankets there.

"It's a home," she said. "Children, mostly. Some families who need a room for a while."

"Do they keep old records?"

"They keep everything. I've been asking for three of our baskets back since winter."

Rae copied the release reference and the name of the house. Then she made another copy, separate from the diagrams, and put it where she could find it without opening the whole folder.

She folded the address around the release reference. The service folder described what was meant to leave the room. At the receiving house, somebody might have written down what arrived.

## 3. What was received

The receiving house had a new roof on an old wall and a bell that rang somewhere nobody seemed to be.

Rae pulled it twice. From inside came a voice telling her that twice did not make the stairs shorter.

Eda opened the door with a child asleep against one shoulder. She read Miri's note, called for someone to take the child and led them through a room still used for meals. The records occupied a cupboard in the thickest wall.

"We moved the books when the first roof went," she said. "Kept the correspondence because people come looking for who looked after them. Or who looked after somebody before them. We don't throw those questions away with the accounts."

Eda found the matching release number between a complaint about spoiled milk and a note about a leaking door. It had a weight beside it, and a carer's name.

"A child?" Rae asked.

"They weren't writing down how much a blanket ate."

Rae showed her a photograph of the shell. Eda put her glasses on, looked at it, then closed the ledger over a strip of paper.

"Find someone who knows what that is. You can come back. The book stays here."

***

Doctor Havel worked in tissue reconstruction at the northern clinic. Rae had asked for her name while having her ribs checked. She paid for a review instead of replacing her torn coat, and brought Havel to the washhouse with the copied receiving reference.

Havel spent longer on the parts Rae had skipped than on the striking whole-body diagram. She asked where the starting material came from, how the batches were distinguished, which sheets were instructions and which recorded completed work.

Rae answered when she could. Miri fetched another lamp when she could not.

At the end, Havel sat on the stair and cleaned her glasses.

"This is not the procedure used for the knee."

"I know that now."

"Good. Then we can stop making it explain the wrong machine."

She pointed to the sequence of stock references.

"This starts with preserved cells. The growth stages are for a complete organism, not a graft. The checks continue through organs that would never be part of a replacement joint. Whatever they called this room later, the installation was made to develop whole human bodies."

Rae looked through the doorway at the towels above the empty cradle.

"Did it work?"

"The service record says some cycles reached release. That doesn't tell me what left the room, or what happened afterwards."

Rae showed her Eda's copied index entry.

Havel put her glasses back on.

***

At the receiving house, Carter stood by the window while the two women bent over the ledger. He had spent the morning making Miri a duplicate key. The old one was too thin at its shoulder; sooner or later it would break inside the shutoff.

The key lay on the table in a small paper packet. Carter kept turning the packet so its edge lined up with the wood.

A.R.I. sat where she could read the enlarged copies without leaning over the original. LaN held a sheet steady for Havel.

The first letter asked for more feeding staff.

The second asked the transport office to stop counting an infant as a standard parcel.

Rae read that one twice.

"There's more," Eda said.

There was no answer attached. A later letter said one of the drivers had stayed to help and asked that his lost time not be taken from his wages.

Carter came away from the window.

Havel compared the source number, the completed growth cycle and the received entry. She checked another. Then a third, from a different delivery.

"These children came out of that programme," she said.

Carter touched the edge of the letter. "Could've been moved from another ward."

Havel turned the service copy so he could see the starting-stock reference carried through to the release number. Eda found the same number in the receiving ledger and placed a strip of paper beneath the line.

He read both. Nobody hurried him.

"They grew people," he said.

Rae had been waiting for someone else to say it. Now that he had, the room did not change. From the next table came the scrape of dried porridge being worked off a bowl. Somewhere outside, two children argued over which of them had found a stick first.

She looked at A.R.I.

"You knew this was possible?"

"The method is new to me."

"But possible."

"I would not have known how to make this apparatus work."

It was not the answer Rae wanted. She could not have said which answer would have helped.

Asher had found her in a room full of children nobody could place. She remembered a coat hung over a broken window, a woman counting bowls, bread in a tin. None of those memories had changed. For a moment she could not reach them without passing through the shape of the empty cradle.

"Does this tell us anything about families now?" Carter asked.

Eda put one hand on the ledger.

"It tells me where some children went. It doesn't tell me where everybody came from."

Rae looked down at the placement names. She had not meant to read them and had read several already.

"We don't need those for this," she said.

Eda laid a blank sheet over the column.

Havel began listing what she would need to check before putting her name to a written opinion. Rae usually found lists reassuring. This one reached her as a series of separate sounds.

"Give me a minute," she said.

Havel stopped.

Carter took the paper packet containing Miri's key and moved it away from the wet ring left by his cup. Then he moved the cup. Then there was nothing left for him to move.

Rae kept her hand beneath the book's spine until Eda was ready to turn the page.

## 4. The authority on the page

They had evidence of one installation. It was A.R.I. who made them write *one* at the top of the sheet.

Carter wanted to take Miri her key. Rae wanted the next volume. Havel wanted to go home and made no apology for it.

Eda returned to the letters about the driver's wages.

"If you want to know who was sending them, that's where I'd look. People stop writing when they're thanked. They keep writing when they're owed money."

The transport reference survived in the corner of the page.

Iva recognised the format, though not the office named above it. She showed Rae a current freight docket. Its account field had more characters, but the old district division still appeared in the middle.

"They moved those records when the depot flooded," she said. "I was on the shift that carried the boxes."

"Where?"

"Records store. Then part of it into the municipal stacks. You need the box list."

"Can you get it?"

"After work. I'm still chasing your night-gate appointment."

Rae looked at the copy she had brought. Iva had a stack of current notices under one arm and a pen mark across her cuff. The freight lane had not become quiet because Rae had found a more interesting question.

"I'll go to the transport office tomorrow," Rae said.

"Take Tom's latest receipts. They lost the last set."

***

Iva's box list took them through the vehicle records to the accounts kept for disputed journeys. The receiving house's letter was filed with the eventual correction to the driver's pay.

"Good," Carter said.

The receiving house had confirmed the delay. The driver's pay had been charged to a restoration transport account. Rae followed that number to the commissioning order and asked for the district returns filed with it.

When they brought them home, Carter cleared the kitchen table and put a bag of hot chestnuts between the papers. Rae took one, burnt her thumb and forgot to eat it.

The founding instruction was not a revelation written for them. Half of it concerned drainage, transport losses and the recurring failure to keep the receiving houses supplied with clean feeding equipment.

The authorising body was named in full: the Original Intelligence's continuity administration. Its recorded authority was carried into the transport ledger, then into acknowledgements from the installations. The office had used the same account across the surviving chain. It had paid for the work, assigned the destinations and answered complaints about the deliveries.

The instruction classified the programme as restoration following the cessation of the war. It named depleted preserved-human stocks and ordered their distribution between several growth facilities rather than keeping the entire reserve in one place.

Rae read the population note beneath it.

The programme was intended to establish viable unmodified human populations alongside the altered survivors. It was not a description of repair to injured adults. The original entrants were listed as stock, the children leaving as people requiring placement.

Carter pushed his chair back. The legs scraped the floor.

"That's what they meant by rebuilding."

Rae had heard that word all her life. Roads. Reservoirs. Houses. The systems that made a city possible. She had pictured people returning to them when the noise stopped.

Here, somebody had specified how many people the houses would need to receive.

She kept her hand on the population note. Asher had never described this room full of children who needed somebody to feed them. She could almost hear the answer they would have given her: another dependency, another way of making humanity unable to leave.

She turned back to the driver's unpaid hours. Whatever else it had been, the receiving house had needed help, and he had stayed.

"That's what this office meant," A.R.I. said.

"You think it's lying?"

"I don't know. We can compare what it ordered with what the other books say happened."

One attachment came from a receiving district, not the continuity office. The writer had crossed out the proposed intake and written beside it: NO STAFF FOR THESE.

Below that, the formal complaint gave way to smaller handwriting.

*My hand was altered to lift things. I can hold a child. I cannot open the feeds you send. The tabs tear off between my fingers and someone else has to do it. The woman opening them for me is due back at the clinic. Her own treatment has already been put off twice.*

*Send the refillable bottles with broad screw caps we asked for. Send someone for the night shift. Please stop counting her as available while she is away.*

Carter turned back to the supply list. The bottles had been entered under replacement equipment, with no priority marked beside them.

A later schedule reduced the intake.

Rae put the two pages beside each other. This was not the triumphant restoration account she had expected after the first heading. The machine had ordered a future. People had had to keep it alive through the night.

The Estuary receiving house's return was pinned to a request for replacement feeding equipment. Fourteen infants had arrived from an installation with a different facility number. Their release references used the same stock-and-cycle system as Miri's records. The receiving clerk had written a weight beside each one, then corrected the transport invoice: fourteen children, not fourteen boxes. One needed a warmed cot the schedule had omitted.

A later return recorded the transfer of that child to a carer near the district clinic. The number followed him through both entries. A.R.I. put the pages beside the commissioning schedule. The administration had ordered that delivery; the house had recorded people arriving and continued accounting for them afterwards.

Other installations had no receiving returns in the collection. The list could not tell them how many of those had worked. A.R.I. left those columns empty.

"More than this city," she said at last. "I can't give you a total from these."

Rae found a later amendment restricting changes to the programme's central authority. Local offices could report need. They could not appoint another coordinating intelligence.

Carter leaned over her shoulder.

"That sounds familiar."

The restriction did not say why it had been imposed. It had no voice, no face, no figure waiting beyond glass to explain itself. It was an instruction carried forward by offices that had needed food and transport and had nowhere else to send the request.

Rae looked at the words *Original Intelligence* again.

Asher had said it took control. They had said human beings were made dependent on things they could not repair or refuse. Rae could hear them saying it in the training room, with a cup balanced on the arm of their chair.

They had also locked the factory shutters.

She put the amendment beside the letters asking for more feeding staff. Neither page cancelled the other.

"Do we know if it still runs any of these places?" she asked.

"Not from this," A.R.I. said.

Carter turned the service photograph towards them. Mineral had grown across the empty cradle's lower ports. Miri's laundry pipe crossed the place where another feed had once entered it.

"We know what this one's doing now."

Rae watched his finger stop at the new pipe.

They still owed Miri the key.

## 5. The work left over

Miri tried the new key twice, then put the old one on a hook inside the inspection cover.

"For when somebody loses this one," she said.

"That one's about to break."

"Then they'll have to be careful with it."

Carter took it off the hook. Miri held out her hand. He looked at the thin shoulder, then at her.

"I'll make another."

"Good."

Upstairs, the first warm water was running. A woman touched the sunburst before filling her jug. Miri waited until she had finished, then sat down with Rae at the dry end of the counter.

Rae had brought Havel's written opinion, copies of the relevant ledger entries with the placement columns covered, and the transport records. Not a headline. Not yet.

Miri read slowly. Once she got up to answer a question about a missing towel. When she returned, she found her place without help.

"You want to tell people," she said.

"Yes."

"They'll want to come down here."

Rae looked towards the stairs. A trolley blocked part of the passage. The morning washing had not finished.

"They will."

"And take pictures of the cupboard. And tell me what my building really is."

Rae had no useful denial ready.

"We could put the records somewhere else for people to read. Make measured drawings of the installation."

"That'll stop some of them."

"Some."

Miri tapped the sunburst at the top of the oldest copy.

"Does this say anything about that?"

"The service book says it was a manufacturer's mark. It doesn't tell us who first prayed to it. Or why they kept doing it."

Miri looked up at the large symbol above the token tray.

"My mother touched it before she opened the water. Her mother too. Sometimes the water didn't come. They still had to get it coming."

She put the page down.

"I don't want the lower room cleared while people are using the upstairs."

"Neither do I," Carter said from the cupboard.

"You're not the one who'll be here when somebody important wants a photograph."

His head appeared round the door.

"Fair point."

Miri crossed the morning hours off the proposed visiting notice and wrote her own contact number beneath them.

"Start with that. We'll see how many come."

Rae took the amended notice. Havel would put her name to the biological finding; the transport and receiving records would accompany it. The account would describe a reconstruction programme operated by the Original Intelligence's administration, with children received alive in separate districts. It would not tell everybody who their ancestors were.

Rae had come to look at a pump. She folded the notice carefully enough that Miri's number stayed on the outside.

Outside, A.R.I. waited beside the repaired washhouse trolley while Carter tightened its loose wheel. He had found the wobble while carrying the folder and had complained about it on every visit.

Rae sat on the low wall next to her.

"Does this change what you think of us?"

A.R.I. turned her head.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You might have been talking to people made by something like you all this time."

A.R.I. looked through the open door at the attendant sorting tokens.

"Not something I know how to do."

"That's not what I meant either."

They sat without speaking while Carter tried a second washer on the axle.

Rae had expected to feel cheated. She did, partly. There were stories Asher had told her that now seemed smaller than the room they had been told in. There were questions she had not known to ask while they were alive.

She could not ask them now. The discovery had given her another way to miss a person she was still angry with.

"I keep wanting to take this to them," she said.

Carter stopped turning the spanner.

"Asher?"

She nodded.

"I want to hear them tell me which part I've got wrong. Then I remember what they said when I asked them about Clona."

A.R.I. kept her hands on her knees. She did not tell Rae what Asher would have said.

Carter tested the wheel. It turned without catching. He spun it once more, then put the spanner away.

"You can want the conversation," he said.

Rae watched him straighten with one hand against his back. She knew which conversation he wanted.

From the doorway, Miri asked if the trolley was finished. Carter pushed it towards her. She loaded the first basket before he had quite let go of the handle.

On the way home, Rae stopped at the transport office with Tom's appeal receipts. The clerk who took them tried to direct her to a different counter. She stayed until he wrote down where they would go next.

Carter went on ahead with A.R.I. At the workshop, he put the photographs of the lower installation in an empty parts drawer. He labelled it with the washhouse address, not the name of a discovery.

Carter left Rupert's bench as it was and took Miri's worn key to his own vice.

There was another copy to make before somebody had to use the broken one.

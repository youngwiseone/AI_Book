# Chapter 5: Coffee, Doubt, and Rupert

By lunchtime the cold had settled into the workshop. Carter had barely slept after Rupert left. He shut the doors and went to meet him.

He pulled his denim jacket tighter around himself and hunched his shoulders as he walked down the gusty alley toward the station, one hand jammed into a pocket, the other wrapped around a hot pastry in thin paper. The thing was full of spiced vegetables and synthetic cheese that stuck to the roof of his mouth, but it was hot and cheap and from the stall outside the platform, which made it respectable.

He bit into the pastry and nearly scorched his tongue. Above him, the platform speakers announced the afternoon route times in A.R.I.'s calm voice. Carter checked the board anyway.

Rupert was already waiting at the other end of the station concourse when Carter arrived, standing beside a cafe cart in a charcoal coat and polished shoes that had no business being near train grime. He held two takeaway cups in one hand and a paper bag in the other, waving as if they were meeting for leisure instead of a catastrophe.

"You are much earlier than expected," Rupert said when Carter reached him. "I had budgeted at least twelve extra minutes for stubbornness."

"Couldn't sleep."

Rupert's face softened for half a second, then smoothed over again. "I brought coffee. And because I know your standards are morally compromised, I also brought fried dough twists from Binjo."

Carter took the bag first. "Good. See? That's why I keep you around."

"You have not kept me around for years."

"Technicality."

They boarded the city transport just as the doors chimed closed. Inside, the carriage smelled of damp coats, battery heat, and someone's aggressively herbal lunch. Rupert chose a pair of seats near the window and set the coffees between them before Carter could slosh one into his own lap.

Carter looked suspiciously into his cup. "What did you do to this?"

"Improved it."

"You sweetened it."

"I am not sure whether you normally drink coffee or the engine degreaser you keep in the kitchen."

Carter grunted and took a sip anyway. It was sweeter than he would have chosen, but not offensively so. He balanced the coffee on one knee, opened the paper bag, and found the dough twists, still warm enough to justify faith in humanity.

Outside, the city slid by in ordered layers.

Thin daylight picked out the wet steel edges of bridges and transit rails. Delivery drones moved in disciplined currents overhead. Walkways filled with workers in staggered rhythms. Here and there old buildings still hunched between newer construction, their facades patched with conduit and ceramic panelling like old bones pinned into modern armour. Banners had gone up overnight over several overpasses: civic condolences, factory memorial notices, official promises of accountability printed in expensive typefaces standing out in pale clean serenity against the otherwise grimy district.

The first public mourning wall near the central line was already crowded with flowers, candles, handwritten names, and projected memorial glyphs. Carter looked away before the train slowed enough to make the details visible.

Rupert watched him for a moment, then reached into his satchel and laid a thin packet on the seat between them.

"This is the public communications brief," he said. "Or what is left of it after I removed the especially insulting phrases."

Carter flicked the top page with two fingers. "How generous."

"My team handles implementation and messaging around A.R.I.'s civic systems. The official line at the moment is tragic systems failure pending investigation. No details about override chains. No details about locked exits. No details about how much the council is panicking."

"They always need someone or something to blame," Carter said.

"Yes," Rupert replied. "And if they do not produce an explanation quickly, the blame starts climbing toward the people who introduced A.R.I., funded her expansion, and assured the public she was safer than human management."

Carter looked out the window again. "So the machine takes the fall and the people who put it there don't."

Rupert's silence answered for him.

For a while the train hummed under them and neither spoke. Carter finished the first dough twist and brushed sugar from his beard with the back of his wrist. Rupert, too civilised to fight for the remaining pastry, only watched with a kind of resigned disgust that suggested long practice.

"You can have one," Carter said eventually, nodding at the leftover piece.

Rupert sighed. "The offer is appreciated, but I prefer not to eat something that looks like it lost a duel."

Rupert reached for the inner pocket of his coat. "There is something else." He produced a small wafer and laid it on Carter's palm.

"I've been going through Chel's old build records," he said. "Notes, voice logs, test comments, half-finished observations, duplicated backups. Hundreds of these things. She recorded everything while working on A.R.I., then left it all in the kind of organisational system that only made sense to her."

Carter's fingers closed around the wafer before he consciously meant them to.

"That sounds less like a system and more like a workbench," he said, examining the wafer.

"Yes," Rupert said. "I assume she learned archival discipline from you."

Rupert held his hand out for the wafer, which Carter handed over, then he pushed it against the personal unit embedded in his wrist. The wafer activated with a soft blue glow, and a half-corrupted voice print unfolded between them in a low private cone of sound.

_If something ever appears wildly out of character in A.R.I.'s decision chains, check the source inputs first. She is designed to reason from what she is given. If the inputs are poisoned, the conclusions will be too._

"That one seemed relevant," Rupert said quietly once the clip ended. "There are dozens more in the same vein. Design habits. Ethics notes. Reminder logs. Observations on bias filtering. Most of them sound like Chel talking to herself while forgetting the recorder was still on."

He handed the wafer back to Carter without comment.

Chel's voice. Frayed by compression, but hers.

Carter shut his eyes for a second, the wafer closed tight in his hand. Hearing her like that, even through distortion and age, felt like being thrown a piece of her he had not known the world had left behind.

When he opened them again, the city had continued without his permission. People still crossed bridges. Trains still ran. Somewhere above ground, memorial walls were still growing flower by flower while officials practised sympathy in front of cameras.

He slipped the wafer into his pocket.

"That was the clearest copy," Rupert said.

Carter kept his hand in his pocket with the wafer.

Rupert reached into his satchel again. This time he brought out a folded sheet.

Carter saw Chel's name at the top.

"What's that?"

"Part of the incident review."

"I've got the report."

"You've got the final summary. So had I."

Carter left the sheet where it was. He remembered the words beneath her name. Software error. A short account of work he hadn't understood, written by people who had stopped answering him once the account was final.

Rupert unfolded the bottom edge. Two entries carried the same review number. The first referred the cause for further examination. The second closed the review with software failure entered as the cause.

"Where's the examination?" Carter asked.

"It isn't in the file they gave me."

"That doesn't mean they didn't do it."

"No."

"I asked for the supporting work," Rupert said. "Not a repeat of the verdict."

The reverse carried a request history. Carter read the dates. The first was from Rupert's first year in government service. There were long gaps, then several entries close together. Most ended with the name of another office.

"This is why you went there?"

Rupert rested both hands on his knees.

"Part of it."

Carter looked up. "You let me think you'd gone to work for the people who—"

He stopped before he said it. A passenger across the aisle glanced towards them, then back at her own screen.

"I did go to work for them," Rupert said. "That part wasn't a misunderstanding."

"You could have told me what you were doing."

"I wanted to have something to show you."

Carter turned the sheet over again. All those dates, and two lines that did not explain the space between them.

"You think they killed her."

Rupert watched the passing window for a moment.

"I went looking for proof that they had. I haven't found it. This could be work they lost. It could be a decision nobody wanted to defend. It isn't proof of murder."

"But you're still looking."

"Yes."

Carter pushed the sheet back towards him.

"Then stop doing it on your own."

Rupert's hand stopped above it.

"I am not asking you to come into those offices."

"Good. They wouldn't enjoy it."

"Carter."

"Bring the copies round. The questions too. I might not know what half the words mean, but I can read a date."

Rupert lowered his hand to the seat.

"You haven't wanted their paperwork in the house."

"I haven't wanted much in the house. You've noticed."

The train braked. Carter caught Rupert's cup before it slid off the seat and set it nearer the window.

"Do you think Clona's connected?"

"I haven't found anything that connects them."

Carter looked at the request dates again. "You were planning to do all of it without telling me."

Rupert had no answer to that.

Carter folded the sheet along its old creases and put it inside his coat, separate from the Clona brief.

"And I didn't only stay because of Chel," Rupert said.

Carter looked at him.

"There are people who ring my office because every other number has sent them somewhere else. Sometimes I can actually get them an answer. I like that part of the job."

"You could do that somewhere better."

"Possibly. I haven't found it yet."

Carter took the remaining piece of dough from the bag and broke it in two. He held out the less damaged half. Rupert accepted it without inspecting the sugar on Carter's fingers.

For a while they ate without speaking. Chel's wafer pressed against the paper inside Carter's coat. Neither could tell him what had happened to her.

"Blade asked for you because of the hardware," Rupert said. "I asked to come along. This isn't why you're here."

"I agreed last night."

"I know."

"You could still have told me last night."

Rupert looked down at his hands. Carter left him to answer that in his own time.

The train shifted lines and curved into the central districts.

"You all right?" Rupert asked.

"No," Carter said. "But I'm on a train with you heading toward a giant state-funded machine shrine, so let's not aim too high."

Rupert nodded. He picked up his cup, then put it down again without drinking.

By the time they reached the government interchange, the weather had turned from merely cold to pointedly hostile. Wind tunneled between the buildings and snapped at coat hems. Rupert led them through a side entrance marked for internal staff, past two layers of guards, through one security hall that smelled faintly of disinfectant and polished stone.

The lift carried them down into the Hive. Carter watched the level markers descend. Somewhere below was what Chel had spent most of her life creating, and what he might now have to take apart.

Rupert was still working on his personal unit.

"They want a press statement this afternoon," he said. "I am supposed to help provide it."

"And are you?"

"Depends what they ask me to say."

A message opened. Rupert read it twice, then closed it without replying.

Beyond the lift, workers crossed the terraces around great trunks of cable and coolant. Carter caught sight of a welder crouched beside an unfinished mounting before Rupert steered him into an inward-bound pod. He wanted to stop and ask what they were installing. A conversation about brackets would have been easier than this.

His palms had started to sweat by the time they reached the centre. Rupert noticed and politely looked elsewhere.

"She doesn't bite."

"It's never the biting I worry about."

They followed the sound of water into a softly lit corridor. Chel had wanted people to feel welcome here. Carter remembered arguing about the expense of it while she cleared a space for the plans on his workbench.

Which was why seeing the chamber for the first time hit him so hard.

Not because of the room.

Because of A.R.I.

He rounded the final curve of the corridor in front of Rupert, wanting to get this over and done with, then stopped so abruptly Rupert had to catch himself to avoid smacking into the back of Carter. Across the chamber, framed by suspended data and all that carefully composed calm, stood a figure that, while not Chel, carried enough of Chel in the tilt of the head, the manner of standing, the exact courtesy of attention, that for one stupid second his body made the wrong recognition and believed the dead could be reversed.

He heard himself inhale.

A.R.I. turned toward him.

She looked puzzled, and in that expression too there was an echo. Not a replica. Not some grotesque copy. Just influence. Design choices. Habits learned from a creator who had spent too much of herself making something capable of gentleness.

That upset him more than a replica would have.

Then he saw the data hanging in the air between A.R.I., Rae, and Blade. Saw the neat command chains, the clean blue abstractions, the whole impossible mess being discussed like enough software could talk a disaster into honesty.

No.

Absolutely not.

He strode into the room before Rupert could warn him off and jabbed a finger toward the projections. "Talking to that thing is going to get you nowhere. Stop asking the software what it thinks happened."

Blade exhaled through his nose. "Carter..."

Carter ignored him and fixed on Rae instead. He did not know her yet, but she looked like someone more likely to understand bad news if it arrived blunt.

"Show me the factory," he said. "Real valves, real locks, real dead hardware. If something murdered those people, the machine logs are not where it started."

Rae did not move towards the door.

"Which lock would you start with?"

"The one that didn't open."

"There's more than one."

She brought two lines out of the suspended display and set them beside each other. Carter could read enough to see the repeated command and the different authority fields.

"This record carries two authority chains," she said. "We don't know what accepted the second one."

He looked at the second line again. There would have been something at the factory to receive it. A controller. A bridge. Not just words in the air.

"Keep that one," he said, pointing. "I want to know what it was plugged into."

A.R.I. enlarged it.

Rupert came up beside him. Carter moved over without looking away from the display.

"We'll need site access," Rae said.

Before A.R.I. could answer, the chamber doors opened again.

Two council aides entered in dark formal coats, followed by a security officer whose expression suggested he would have preferred to escort a bomb. The lead aide did not look at Carter or Rae at all. He addressed only A.R.I.

"A.R.I., a hearing has been convened," he said. "You will attend as soon as the secured council circuit is ready."

Even Carter could feel the shift in the room.

A.R.I. went still. "This hearing was not entered into my schedule."

"It has been authorised at council priority."

"For what purpose?"

The aide smoothed one cuff. "Review of your conduct and operational liability regarding Clona. On transfer, you are to shut down all outer appearances, inputs, and outputs until further notice. No external communication will be permitted during proceedings."

That landed badly enough that even Rupert stopped trying to look decorative.

Rae's mouth tightened. "We need access to the factory."

"That decision is above your clearance," the aide snapped, as if speaking to a bothersome insect.

Blade looked from the aide to A.R.I. His composure slipped. The concern was brief but real. He had not expected this either.

A.R.I.'s gaze moved once between Rae and Carter.

"I will attend," she said. "The receiving circuit is not available."

The aide checked his slate. For a moment Carter enjoyed watching him discover that an order was not the same thing as a working connection.

"Remain here until it is. You will receive the transfer instruction directly."

"How long?" Rae asked.

"That is not for me to determine."

The aides left with the security officer. A.R.I. remained beside the desk, one hand resting on its edge. Carter had seen Chel stand like that when somebody called her away from a job she meant to finish.

He looked at the projections instead.

Blade waited until the doors sealed behind them before moving. Then he crossed the chamber in three quick strides and lowered his voice.

"I can give you service access to Clona," he said.

Rae looked at him sharply. "I thought that was above our clearance."

"It is." Blade was already working at his wrist unit. "Which is why I am overriding it."

Carter eyed him. "And this won't come back on you?"

Blade gave a tired, humourless look. "At this point, that is no longer near the top of my concerns."

Rupert exhaled softly, tension and relief arriving together.

"Service access expires in three hours and twenty-nine minutes," Blade said. "After that, you are trespassers. Find what you can before the official story closes over it."

Rae glanced down at LaN. "Tell me you kept the session cache."

LaN's display brightened. "Multiple redundancies. The command chain, timing discrepancies, and chamber notes are all backed up locally."

"Good," Rae said.

Carter was still looking at the pressure traces. One climbed in a smooth line while the others shook.

"That gauge. Can you bring it closer?"

A.R.I. enlarged the trace. Somewhere else in the building, people were preparing to take this room away from her. For the moment, it still worked.

# Reconstruction scene trials

**Optional prose experiments, not manuscript events.** Read with `Discovery sequence.md`. These are two non-contiguous scenes, not a complete discovery compressed into one visit. Miri, the building and records are proposed. The preceding false interpretation, acquisition of independent receiving records and external technical review still need scenes. These trials establish, provisionally, a particular programme producing living humans, not yet its commission by the Original AI, post-war purpose or population scale. No character's ancestry is established.

## Trial A: the line that still works

*Return visit after the knee interpretation has failed. The moved cover and service index are already known; another leaking joint is part of the work they still owe the community.*

Miri put a bucket beneath the leaking joint and watched Carter look at it.

"That's the third one," she said.

"Bucket?"

"Joint. I'm not counting buckets."

He crouched. Somebody had wrapped the pipe in cloth and painted over the cloth. Water had found the weave and was following it down to the floor.

"You've made it absorbent."

"My predecessor made it absorbent. I've made it your problem."

Rae held the service drawing against the wall. According to it, the pipe ended two rooms behind them. According to the floor, it did not.

A.R.I. stood clear of the puddle with the lamp. She had offered to hold the drawing; Carter had given her the thing he most needed kept steady.

"This branch is marked out of service," Rae said.

"Laundry," Miri said.

"Now?"

"Right now."

Carter put his cutter away.

They followed the pipe to an inspection chamber beneath the stairs. The cover bore a sunburst, polished bright at its centre by hands that had touched it on the way past. Miri opened her ring of keys and selected one by feel.

Inside, a much smaller fitting sat crooked beneath the cover. The service number had been punched into its own metal, clear of the emblem.

Carter checked the fitting's number against the corrected drawing Miri had let him copy. This time they matched.

"Still an elaborate lid," he said.

"People kept touching the old handle. Now they touch that."

She waited until he had checked the return connection before closing it. Carter wrote it on the back of the drawing, then asked where the return went.

Miri took them down another flight.

The old room beyond had once been much larger. New brick walls cut it into cells, each filled with folded blankets, soap cartons or laundry trolleys. Between two walls stood a curved shell tall enough for a person. Its front was missing. Layers of narrow channels ran through the exposed lining.

A.R.I. raised the lamp.

"Don't pull that trolley out," Miri said. "It holds the board over the drain."

Rae stepped round it. Small tool marks lined the shell's inner edge. Someone had worked on it repeatedly, each new cut stopping just short of the channels.

She had seen something like the lining in Asher's diagrams. Not this arrangement. Not this size.

"What did this room do?"

"Before blankets? Depends which inventory you read."

Miri lifted a carton from a narrow shelf. Under it lay a metal folder too heavy to be convenient. She opened it on the trolley without moving the trolley itself.

The first sheet carried the sunburst. The drawing beneath it showed a different machine. The next sheet was a replacement, punched by hand to fit the old rings.

Rae turned it slowly.

Whole-body growth sequence. Tissue supports, feed paths, a release procedure that did not begin with removing a graft from its mould.

She checked the following page. Then the one after that.

Carter glanced over her shoulder. "Another knee?"

"No."

She said it too quickly. She read the headings again.

"I don't think so."

A.R.I. lowered the lamp until the numbered channels in the drawing cast small shadows across the paper. Rae followed one with her finger, then looked at the shell.

"Could you light the bottom?"

The matching number was still visible beneath a patch of old adhesive.

Above them, a pipe knocked three times.

Miri looked towards the stairs. "They've finished the first wash. We can close the branch now."

Carter had not moved.

"Carter," she said.

He looked down at the tools in his hand, then up at Rae.

"We need this room left as it is," Rae said.

"You need my washroom dry," Miri replied. "That was what we agreed."

Rae closed the folder. The rings did not line up; she had to ease the last one through the paper instead of forcing it.

"Can I come back to this?"

"After the joint. And write down which pages you copy."

Carter followed her to the inspection chamber. A.R.I. took the lamp with them. Rae stayed until the light had gone, looking at the shape the shell made against the new brick wall.

Then she went up to hold the bucket.

## Trial B: what was received

The receiving-house ledger would not lie flat.

Rae kept one hand beneath its spine while the keeper fitted a padded wedge under the other side. They had already told her not to press it. She had nearly done it twice.

The room was still used for meals. From the next table came the sound of someone scraping dried porridge off a bowl.

Carter stood beside the window. A.R.I. sat where she could read the enlarged copies without leaning over the original.

These records had not come from Miri's folder. They belonged to the people who had accepted deliveries from the building, argued about them and asked for things that never arrived.

The first letter wanted more feeding staff.

The second wanted the transport office to stop counting an infant as a single standard parcel.

Rae read that one twice.

"Read the answer," the keeper said.

There was no answer. There was a later letter, in the same hand, saying one of the drivers had stayed to help and asking that his lost time not be taken from his wages.

Carter moved away from the window.

Rae found the facility code in the margin. It matched the shell inventory. Beside it, another hand had added the infants' received weights, feeding difficulties and the names of the carers taking them home.

The procedure copies lay on the table beside the ledger. The biological review had separated ordinary graft work from the complete growth protocol. Whole bodies, begun from preserved human cell stock. The apparatus could have been an abandoned experiment. These entries described people arriving alive.

Rae looked from one set of pages to the other.

"Not replacement limbs," she said.

"No," A.R.I. said.

"And not just a plan."

"These records describe children being received."

Carter touched the edge of the copied letter. "Could have been moved from another ward."

Rae turned it so he could see the source field and the release reference beneath it. The same reference appeared against the completed growth cycle in the service record. He read it. She did not help him reach the end.

"They grew people," he said.

The keeper looked up from the padded wedge.

"Apparently."

Rae had expected the word to meet something. An argument. A correction. Instead the person at the next table kept scraping the bowl.

"How long have you had this?"

"The book? Longer than I've had this job."

"Did you know what it meant?"

"I know it means they needed more staff. You brought the other half."

Rae looked at the letters again. Her eyes kept returning to the word *received*, as if the writer had accepted something too large with too small a word.

She thought of the shelter Asher had taken her from. Of the bread tin. Of all the times they had said they were keeping humanity alive, and how readily she had decided she knew what they meant.

That did not put the workers back outside Clona. It did not change what had been done to the man in the factory. She gripped the ledger's spine too hard and loosened her hand.

"Do we know who their families are now?" Carter asked.

"Not from this," Rae said.

The keeper moved a loose page away from the open book.

"And you aren't taking the placement names."

"The totals and the source references," she said. "May we copy those?"

The keeper considered it, then fetched another sheet to cover the rest of the page.

A.R.I. turned one of the procedure copies face down so it would not touch the old binding.

"There are things this does not establish," she said.

"I know," Rae said.

A.R.I. waited.

Rae looked at her. "I don't know. Not yet. Just give me a minute."

Nobody spoke for a while.

Carter took the blank sheet Rae had brought for notes. Instead of writing a heading across it, he put it beneath the wet ring his cup had left on the table.

Rae kept her hand under the book's spine until the keeper was ready to turn the page.
